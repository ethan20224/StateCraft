# If I were running Singapore: an AI strategy built on hard choices

I'm reading "genuinely matter" to mean leverage and value capture: other countries and companies need Singapore and pay for it. I'm not reading it as prestige or as having a famous model. I'm also writing as if I had full political room to act, but inside Singapore's real limits: 6 million people, roughly 700 km² of land, and power that comes almost entirely from imported gas.

## Start with the uncomfortable fact

Singapore will not win the AI race. Any strategy that tries to win it is the one that ends up as a PDF. Frontier AI is a scale game: hundreds of thousands of accelerators, gigawatts of power, tens of billions of dollars per model generation, and thousands of top researchers. Singapore's newest national supercomputer, ASPIRE 2B, launched in June with [about 1,500 Nvidia H200s for S$270 million](https://mothership.sg/2026/06/singapore-supercomputer-aspire-2b/). That is a real national asset and a rounding error next to a frontier lab's training cluster. Matching the US or China on that axis isn't ambitious. It's a category error.

The more urgent fact is what happened nine days ago. On 16 November, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security [named Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil as countries with an "unacceptable risk of diversion"](https://aljazeera.com/economy/2026/11/17/us-moves-to-block-chinas-remote-access-to-ai-chips-amid-nvidia-scandal). Shipping advanced AI chips to Singapore now needs a US export licence. The same rule creates a [worldwide control on data centres that give more than 12 million aggregate TPP of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other D:5 countries](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/international-compliance-blog/bis-targets-data-center-remote-access-and-imposes-steeper-requirements-on-foundries-and-osats.html), with roughly 90 days for existing facilities to get licensed.

The trigger was Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud firm that [reports say funnelled billions of dollars of Nvidia hardware to Chinese firms through regional subsidiaries](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/sg-us-ai-chip-controls-southeast-asia/). US prosecutors have now confirmed a criminal investigation. The scandal is big enough that [reports say it has already cost Jensen Huang the Nvidia CEO job](https://www.malaymail.com/amp/news/singapore/2026/11/17/singapore-scrambles-to-protect-its-reputation-as-nvidia-ai-chip-scandal-deepens/243565). There is a structural reason Washington sees Singapore as a conduit. In Nvidia's fiscal 2025, [18% of its revenue was billed to Singapore, but under 2% was actually shipped there](https://fourweekmba.com/nvidia-singapore-revenue-tracker/). Singapore was working as an invoicing node for compute that went elsewhere.

So the starting point isn't "how do we join the AI race." It's this: **the world's most important AI supplier has just formally classified Singapore as a leakage risk, and Singapore's whole economic model rests on being the most trusted place in Asia.** A strategy that doesn't start there is decoration.

## What Singapore actually has, and what actually limits it

The assets are real but specific. Singapore has a state that can carry out policy at a speed few democracies match. It is a heavy AI user: Anthropic's Economic Index ranked it [second in the world for per-capita usage, behind only Israel](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report). It holds a meaningful piece of the physical AI supply chain. Micron's [US$7 billion HBM packaging plant starts production in 2027, a separate US$24 billion NAND fab starts wafer output in late 2028, and Micron already makes 98% of its flash memory here](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/01/27/micron-plans-24-billion-memory-chipmaking-plant-in-singapore). Frontier labs are arriving: OpenAI has [committed more than S$300 million, Google signed a national AI partnership, and NVIDIA is opening a research lab focused on embodied and efficient AI](https://www.edb.gov.sg/en/news-and-insights/singapore-updates-national-ai-strategy-partners-google-and-openai). Singapore also has policy credibility in governance. Its agentic AI framework, [launched at Davos in January](https://www.mddi.gov.sg/newsroom/singapore-launches-new-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai--/), was the first serious government guide for enterprises deploying agents. And GIC and Temasek are among the largest pools of patient capital in the world.

The constraints are harder than the official documents admit.

**Energy is the binding constraint.** About 95% of Singapore's electricity comes from gas. Peak demand was [8.6 GW in August 2026 and could reach 9.9 to 13 GW by 2031](https://ema.gov.sg/news-events/news/media-releases/2026/five-year-infrastructure-plan-to-meet-unpredecented-future-demand), driven partly by chips and data centres. The plan for clean power imports is slipping. Wood Mackenzie expects imports to reach [about 15% of the mix by 2035, roughly half the one-third target. Of 9.25 GW of approved import capacity, none has reached financial close](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/business/economy/2026/10/1550070/singapore-set-miss-2035-green-power-import-target). Data centres already use [about 7% of national electricity, projected to reach 12% by the end of the decade](https://technode.global/2026/07/31/singapore-tightens-data-center-growth-as-johor-bangkok-jakarta-race-ahead/).

**The talent pool is small.** Even with every incentive, Singapore cannot produce the volume of top researchers that the US or China can. Its resident fertility rate below 1.0 means the domestic workforce is shrinking, not growing.

**Trust is now damaged.** That is the asset everything else depends on.

### What the May 2026 refresh gets right, and what it leaves out

The [May 2026 refresh of the National AI Strategy](https://mothership.sg/2026/05/jo-teo-national-ai-strategy-update/) gets the basic instinct right. It puts adoption ahead of model-building, through missions in advanced manufacturing, finance, connectivity and healthcare (sectors that make up over 40% of GDP), a programme to move 10,000 enterprises into real AI use, and a [National AI Council chaired by the Prime Minister](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366638920/Singapore-to-form-National-AI-Council-expands-tax-breaks-to-ease-AI-adoption).

What it doesn't do is choose. It has ten refreshed priorities, and ten priorities is the same as none. It says almost nothing about the three questions that will decide Singapore's AI future:

- Where Singapore stands when Washington and Beijing force a choice at the hardware layer.
- How Singapore rations power it doesn't have.
- What Singapore will deliberately stop doing.

A real strategy is mostly a list of things you've decided not to pursue, plus the resources and accountability to execute what's left.

## The core thesis: be the trusted interface, not a contender

Look at how power actually builds up in a technology stack. It goes to three kinds of players:

- those who control chokepoints (TSMC, ASML, Nvidia);
- those who are trusted intermediaries between systems that don't trust each other;
- those who turn the technology into productivity fastest.

Singapore's entire history is the second role. It became the port, the financial centre, the arbitration seat and the regional headquarters by being the place where parties who don't fully trust each other can meet under rules both sides can check. The AI-era version of that role is clear.

So the strategy has three bets, sitting on three enabling layers.

**The three bets:**

1. Become the most verifiably trusted compute jurisdiction in Asia.
2. Become the world's reference deployment, meaning the country that proves AI at whole-of-nation scale and then sells that operating know-how abroad.
3. Deepen Singapore's hold on the parts of the physical AI stack where it already matters: memory, advanced packaging, equipment, and efficient compute for hot climates.

**The three enabling layers:** energy, talent, and governance that actually executes.

**What Singapore explicitly won't do:**

- Build a sovereign frontier model to compete with US or Chinese labs.
- Fight Johor, Jakarta or Bangkok for raw megawatts.
- Measure success by courses completed or MoUs signed.

## The US-China question: layered alignment, not neutrality

This is where most commentary goes wrong. Singapore's stated doctrine is "we don't choose sides." In practice it has always been aligned differently at different layers: security cooperation with the US, deep trade and investment with China, and principled consistency on international law. The new BIS rule doesn't end that approach. It ends ambiguity at one specific layer, the hardware and compute layer, where Washington now prices ambiguity as risk.

My answer is layered alignment, set out explicitly.

**At the chip and compute layer, align with US rules.** There is no realistic substitute for American accelerators in serious AI for at least the next several years. Singapore's semiconductor base runs on US tools and US customers. The rule also binds the companies operating in Singapore whatever Singapore's own law says. Fighting it gains nothing. Being lumped in with higher-risk jurisdictions costs a lot.

The rule also builds a two-tier market that hurts Singapore's own champions most. [US-headquartered clouds like AWS, Microsoft and Google are effectively carved out, while local and non-US operators face licensing](https://asianews.network/?p=315104). In practice, ST Telemedia, Keppel and Singtel's data centre business now compete with one hand tied while their American rivals don't. Getting country-level trusted status is therefore an industrial policy priority, not just a diplomatic one.

**At the application, commerce and talent layer, stay fully open to China.** Chinese companies should remain welcome for regional headquarters, commerce, research on uncontrolled technology, and talent. Singapore should not ban Chinese open-weight models. It should keep Chinese capital and people as part of the ecosystem. That openness is part of Singapore's value to the US as well: a place where Western and Chinese systems meet under verifiable rules is more useful than a place that has simply picked a camp.

**Make the compliance regime symmetrical.** This is the move that keeps the neutrality story intact. Singapore's position until now has been that [it does not enforce other countries' unilateral export controls, but it acts against fraud](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/megaspeed-nvidia-customer-huang-cheating-spy-scandal). I would replace that with a broader principle written into Singapore law: Singapore will not be used as a conduit to get around the export laws of its major trading partners, any of them.

This isn't a fiction designed to placate Beijing. China's own export regime also targets rerouting. Its [MOFCOM Announcement No. 26, in force since July, explicitly lists routing exports through third countries to evade controls as reportable conduct](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/recent-china-export-control-actions-signal-active-enforcement-for-rare-earths-and-strategic-minerals). A regime that protects Chinese rare-earth controls as seriously as American chip controls is defensible in Beijing in a way pure US alignment is not.

**When this fails.** Layered alignment breaks in two scenarios. In the first, Washington keeps moving the goalposts and treats compliance as a floor to raise, not a deal to honour. The [12.5% tariff the US put on a third of Singapore's exports in July](https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/07/25/trump-imposes-new-125-tariff-on-singapore-affecting-one-third-of-exports-to-us) shows that being a close friend doesn't buy immunity. In the second, Beijing reads the compute regime as joining the US camp and retaliates through investment or tourism.

The hedges:

- Make US cooperation two-way as a condition. The Home Affairs ministry has already said [US agencies did little to help Singapore's own investigations despite repeated requests](https://asianews.network/?p=315104), and that is a fair point to push.
- Build relationships at the compute layer with Japan, Korea, the EU, India and the Gulf, so no single partner holds all the cards.
- Keep the application layer visibly open to China, so the alignment stays narrow and legible.

### Fix the national model's dependency

A small but telling point: AI Singapore moved its flagship regional model, SEA-LION, [onto Alibaba's Qwen as its base in late 2025](https://technode.com/2025/11/25/singapores-national-ai-program-drops-meta-model-and-switches-to-alibabas-qwen/). As a technical choice that was defensible, because Qwen was the strongest open model for multilingual Southeast Asia. As a strategic posture it is fragile. The national model should be base-agnostic, with a pipeline that can rebuild SEA-LION on Gemma, Llama, Mistral, OpenAI's open weights or Qwen within weeks. The real sovereign asset is the regional data, the evaluation suite and the fine-tuning capability, not any one base model. Owning those means Singapore is never stuck on someone else's geopolitics.

## Energy: stop pretending Singapore can be a compute superpower

Singapore cannot be a large-scale compute host, and the strategy should say so plainly. The [August allocation of 200 MW to four operators on Jurong Island](https://technode.global/2026/08/21/singapore-allocates-200-mw-of-new-data-center-capacity-to-digital-realty-equinix-keppel-st-telemedia/) drew more than 20 proposals. It also required over 50% green power, liquid cooling and top-tier efficiency. That is the right instinct: treat megawatts as a scarce national resource and hand them out by value, not first-come-first-served.

I'd go further in four ways.

**Allocate megawatts to the highest-value compute.** The criteria should explicitly favour workloads that need to be in Singapore: low-latency inference for regional finance and services, regulated and sovereign workloads, research labs that bring talent, and capacity that serves the region's languages and markets. Bulk training runs that could happen anywhere should be pushed to partner locations. A gigawatt of commodity training in Singapore earns less than 200 MW of strategic inference and research, and it costs more politically every time electricity bills rise.

**Price power at full cost and attach a "compute dividend."** Data centres should pay the true marginal cost of power, including carbon and grid reinforcement. The grid investment programme alone is [up to S$5.3 billion over 2025 to 2029, about 11 times the previous five years](https://ema.gov.sg/news-events/news/media-releases/2026/five-year-infrastructure-plan-to-meet-unpredecented-future-demand). Future allocation rounds should score bidders on whether they reserve a slice of capacity, say 5 to 10%, at cost for national research and startups. That turns scarce land and power into broad national capability, not just rent.

**Own tropical efficiency as an export.** Most of the world's future data centre growth will happen in hot, humid places: Southeast Asia, India, the Gulf, Africa, Latin America. Singapore has already written a tropical data centre standard, and resource-efficient AI is a named pillar of its [S$1 billion-plus national AI R&D plan](https://www.mddi.gov.sg/newsroom/singapore-invests-over-s-1-billion-in-national-ai-research-and-development-plan-to-strengthen-ai-research-capabilities-and-our-position-as-global-ai-hub/). Singapore should aim to be the world's leading lab for running AI efficiently in the tropics, covering cooling, chip-level efficiency, model compression and inference optimisation, and then export the standards, engineering firms and operators. That is a niche where a small country can lead globally, because the bottleneck is engineering talent, not scale.

**Be honest about the timelines.** For this decade, the realistic levers are:

- gas, through [five new gas plants by 2031](https://ema.gov.sg/news-events/news/media-releases/2026/five-year-infrastructure-plan-to-meet-unpredecented-future-demand);
- efficiency;
- the Malaysia corridor, which Wood Mackenzie calls [the only import corridor with a credible path to delivery this decade](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/business/economy/2026/10/1550070/singapore-set-miss-2035-green-power-import-target), plus a second interconnector of up to 2 GW now under study.

Nuclear is a 2035-and-beyond option. Singapore has [made no decision and is still assessing small modular reactors](https://ema.gov.sg/news-events/news/feature-stories/2026/understanding-advanced-nuclear-energy-technologies-how-singapore-is-assessing-its-potential). The Minister has called the energy security challenge ["existential"](https://www.malaymail.com/news/singapore/2026/03/03/singapore-eyes-nuclear-shift-as-energy-crunch-turns-existential-parliament-told/211157). I'd set a hard go/no-go decision date of 2029 and use the time until then to build the regulator, the siting work and the workforce. Studying nuclear indefinitely is how countries end up with neither nuclear power nor credibility.

**The Johor complication.** Johor now has [over 1 GW of live capacity, 0.7% vacancy and an 8.5 GW pipeline](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/business/economy/2026/08/1522642/johor-data-centre-boom-hits-capacity-wall). The obvious plan was "Singapore as control plane, Johor as the factory floor." But Malaysia is on the same BIS list. That turns a problem into an opening: Singapore should offer its compute integrity regime (below) as a joint template for a cross-border trusted compute zone with Johor. If both sides run the same know-your-customer rules, audits and registries, the corridor becomes something Washington can sign off on. Singapore then becomes the compliance hub for the region's compute, the same role it plays for the region's money.

## Chips: memory, packaging and compliance, not a leading-edge fab

Singapore should not chase leading-edge logic manufacturing. That race belongs to Taiwan, the US, Korea and Japan, and the subsidies needed to enter it would buy far more elsewhere. Where Singapore already matters, and where AI demand is exploding, is in memory and advanced packaging. High-bandwidth memory is one of the real bottlenecks in AI hardware. Micron's HBM plant coming online in 2027 puts Singapore inside the most constrained part of the AI supply chain. The next prize is a CoWoS-class advanced packaging line, the step where AI chips are physically assembled with their memory. That should be the top target for the Economic Development Board's semiconductor team over the next three years.

The new BIS rule quietly creates an advantage here. It [tightens due-diligence obligations on foundries and packaging houses for chip designers located in Singapore and the other listed countries](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/international-compliance-blog/bis-targets-data-center-remote-access-and-imposes-steeper-requirements-on-foundries-and-osats.html). If Singapore builds the region's most rigorous verification system, then "packaged in Singapore" becomes a compliance asset as well as a manufacturing one. Over the next decade, trust will be a production input, as real as power and water.

## Talent: density at the top, fluency across the base

The official 15,000-practitioner target was a reasonable 2023 benchmark. It is the wrong metric now. Singapore doesn't win on headcount. It wins on a high concentration of exceptional people, which then attracts more of them. I'd run two separate talent strategies, because they solve different problems.

**At the frontier, target hundreds, not thousands.** Set a goal of bringing 300 to 500 world-class AI researchers and engineers to Singapore over three years, on a package designed for that tier. It would include:

- guaranteed compute, from reserved slices of national capacity and the "compute dividend" above;
- immediate visas with work rights for spouses;
- housing support;
- lab affiliations that come with real research freedom.

The window is unusually good. US visa tightening has made America harder for Asian researchers. Chinese researchers who can't easily go to the US, and don't want to stay home, need a credible third option. Singaporeans working at frontier labs abroad are the most underused talent pool of all, and a targeted return programme with real upside would pay for itself quickly.

The other lever is converting lab partnerships from sales offices into research presence. OpenAI's S$300 million, Google's partnership, Anthropic's office and NVIDIA's lab are good starts. The government should use its strongest card, being an anchor customer at national scale, and offer it in exchange for research headcount and work on regional languages and safety, not just go-to-market teams.

There is a political edge to this. Foreign talent is a sensitive issue, and the frontier programme has to be visibly tied to local outcomes: Singaporean co-researchers, faculty pipelines, and spin-out rights. Done that way, it passes the "who benefits" test.

**At the base, measure adoption depth, not training throughput.** Budget 2026's AI measures include [practical AI training by profession, starting with accountants and lawyers, plus six months of free premium AI tools for people who complete courses](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366638920/Singapore-to-form-National-AI-Council-expands-tax-breaks-to-ease-AI-adoption). The government also aims to [train 100,000 AI-savvy workers by 2029](https://e.vnexpress.net/news/tech/tech-news/singapore-to-train-100-000-ai-savvy-workers-by-2029-5046165.html). Most countries get this wrong. Course completion is easy to count and weakly linked to productivity. What matters is whether core workflows actually change. The national metric should be the share of workers using AI in their main daily tasks, plus measured gains in output per worker within the four mission sectors. If a programme can't show movement on those, it gets cut.

**The social contract.** Singapore's economy leans heavily toward finance, professional services and administration, which are the jobs most exposed to AI. If adoption feels like something done to workers, the politics will turn and slow everything down. The strategy needs a credible promise, funded in advance, that productivity gains will be shared: wage support during transitions, serious retraining, and public-sector savings visibly put back into services. Singapore's high-trust state is an advantage here. It is not a guarantee.

## Deployment: be the world's reference customer

This is where Singapore can lead the world outright. A small, digitally integrated, high-trust state with strong institutions is the ideal place to prove what AI does at national scale. Very few places can run that experiment. The value is twofold. First, AI is how Singapore keeps growing as its workforce shrinks. Second, the know-how is exportable.

I'd set hard, public, outcome-based targets in four domains:

- **Government:** an agent-first public service, where every agency publishes cycle-time and cost targets for its core processes.
- **Healthcare:** AI documentation and triage across the public system, aimed squarely at an ageing population.
- **Logistics:** use AI to run the port and Changi's Terminal 5. T5 is [expected to double airport capacity from 70 million to 140 million passengers](https://www.edb.gov.sg/en/news-and-insights/singapore-updates-national-ai-strategy-partners-google-and-openai), and that cannot be staffed the old way.
- **Finance:** AI in compliance and anti-money-laundering, with the Monetary Authority setting the standards the region copies.

Then sell the operating models. Singapore's government-linked companies already export ports, airports and urban planning. In the next decade they should be exporting AI-run ports, airports and public services to the nearly 700 million people next door, and beyond.

**Procurement is the hidden lever.** The quickest way to grow a local AI ecosystem is for the government to be a fast, demanding first customer. I'd set a 90-day maximum for pilot procurement from startups, with clear paths from pilot to scale. That does more for local founders than another grant scheme.

## Trust as an industry: assurance, insurance and arbitration

This is the bet I think is most underrated. As AI agents take real actions in the economy, the binding constraint on adoption becomes liability. Who pays when an agent makes a costly mistake, and how does anyone know a system is safe enough to deploy? Singapore already has most of the pieces of a global answer:

- the AI Verify testing work and the agentic AI framework;
- one of the world's major reinsurance hubs;
- a leading international arbitration centre;
- a financial regulator that is respected globally.

Joined up, these could make Singapore the place where AI risk is tested, certified, insured and settled.

Concretely:

- Build an AI assurance regime that gives firms a clear liability benefit, a regulatory safe harbour, if they certify their systems through recognised testing.
- Work with reinsurers to build a market for AI liability insurance priced on that testing data.
- Create an arbitration track for AI disputes.
- Lead an aviation-style incident reporting network. Josephine Teo has already [floated this with Norway and Finland](https://asianews.network/?p=315540).

The way this fails is if certification turns into box-ticking, or if major markets don't recognise it. Mutual recognition with the UK, Japan, Korea and US standards bodies has to be part of the build from day one.

## Governance: what makes it not a PDF

A strategy becomes a PDF when it has many priorities, input-based targets, nobody clearly accountable, and no mechanism for stopping things. The fix is structural. The National AI Council should cut the ten priorities to the three bets above plus the enabling layers. It should name a single accountable minister and a small delivery unit reporting directly to the Prime Minister, with authority to move budget between agencies. Every programme should come with a sunset clause and an annual stop-list.

It should also publish a quarterly scorecard with a few outcome metrics, honest enough to show red. Here is roughly what I'd put on it, as my own proposed targets for 2029:

| Metric | Target by 2029 |
|---|---|
| Trusted-jurisdiction status: Singapore-licensed operators treated on par with US-headquartered ones | Achieved by end-2027 |
| Share of workers using AI in core daily tasks (survey plus usage data) | Above 50% |
| Labour productivity growth in the four mission sectors, with AI's contribution estimated openly | Measurably above trend |
| Frontier-lab research staff in Singapore (research, not sales) | 500+ |
| Advanced packaging and HBM: Singapore's share of global capacity | Rising year on year, with one CoWoS-class line committed |
| Revenue earned abroad by Singapore firms from AI-enabled services and infrastructure | Tracked and growing |
| Locally based AI startups raising Series B or later | Tracked and growing |

## The practical sequence

**The next 90 days.** This window matters most, because it lines up with the BIS rule's clock. If the rule was published on schedule around 19 November, the 60-day comment period closes around mid-January, and existing data centres need licences by around mid-February.

1. **File formal comments and send a senior delegation to Washington.** Propose a "trusted jurisdiction" pathway: operators licensed under a verified national regime get treatment equivalent to US-headquartered firms. Back the ask with the whole relationship, not just tech: US chip investments in Singapore, American regional headquarters, and long-standing security cooperation.
2. **Announce, and fast-track, a Strategic Compute Integrity regime.** Build it by extending Singapore's existing Strategic Goods (Control) Act, which keeps it in line with legal tradition. It would include:
   - licensing for operators above a compute threshold that matches US metrics;
   - know-your-customer checks on the ultimate beneficial owners of large compute customers;
   - a serial-level registry of every controlled-class accelerator in the country;
   - physical audits by IMDA and Customs;
   - stiff penalties and whistleblower rewards.
   Write the symmetry principle into the law itself.
3. **Run a national accelerator census within 60 days**, so Singapore can tell Washington precisely what is in the country and where.
4. **Move fast and openly on Megaspeed.** Visible enforcement is worth more than any number of statements.
5. **Open a parallel channel to Beijing** explaining the symmetrical regime and confirming that the application layer stays open.
6. **Give local operators hands-on help with licence readiness** so they aren't wiped out during the transition.
7. **Cut the national strategy down to three bets, name the owner, and publish the first scorecard.**

**Six to eighteen months.**

- Convert lab MoUs into research-and-deployment deals, with the government as anchor customer.
- Reform data centre power pricing and make the next allocation round value-weighted, with a compute dividend.
- Commit to a 2029 nuclear decision and start building the regulator.
- Propose an ASEAN trusted-compute framework to Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia, all of which are now on the same list, with Singapore's regime as the template.
- Launch the frontier talent programme and the diaspora return programme.
- Set agent-first targets for government and roll out AI documentation across public healthcare.
- Rebuild SEA-LION so it can switch base models.

**Eighteen to thirty-six months.**

- Stand up the assurance, insurance and arbitration hub with its first mutual-recognition agreements.
- Land a CoWoS-class advanced packaging investment.
- Publish tropical-compute efficiency standards and start exporting them.
- Hold the first serious scorecard review, and actually close programmes that aren't moving the numbers.

**To 2035.**

- Make the nuclear decision and, if it's a yes, build toward a first unit.
- Develop the longer-term energy cluster now being studied for Pulau Tekong.
- Turn Singapore's robot density, already [about five times the global average](https://www.edb.gov.sg/en/news-and-insights/singapore-updates-national-ai-strategy-partners-google-and-openai), plus the NVIDIA lab and the Punggol physical-AI testbed, into global leadership in AI for logistics and manufacturing.

## The three mistakes this strategy is built to avoid

The first mistake is confusing owning models with having power. Countries that pour money into prestige models end up with expensive assets that go stale within a year. The countries with real leverage are the ones others can't route around.

The second is confusing neutrality with ambiguity. Singapore's neutrality has always been credible because it was principled and rules-based, not because it was vague. Ambiguity at the hardware layer is now being priced as risk. Clarity at that layer is what keeps neutrality possible everywhere else.

The third is confusing activity with outcomes. Training enrolments, MoUs, centres of excellence and strategy refreshes are all activity. Productivity, trusted status, research presence and export revenue are outcomes. Every government says it measures outcomes. Very few will publish a scorecard that can show red, and that willingness is the real test of whether this is a strategy or a PDF.

## One note for you as a founder here

Since you're building a consumer AI product from Singapore, the BIS rule changes little for you directly. App builders who rent compute from US-headquartered clouds and model APIs sit inside the carve-out. The bigger risk would be a slow erosion of Singapore's attractiveness to the capital and talent you'll eventually need, which is why the trust question matters even to people nowhere near a data centre. More immediately, Budget 2026's [400% tax deduction on qualifying AI spend, capped at S$50,000 a year for YA2027 and YA2028](https://www.herworld.com/independence/career/400-cent-tax-deductions-ai-singapores-new-business-incentives-explained), is worth checking against your model and API costs once IRAS publishes the final criteria.
