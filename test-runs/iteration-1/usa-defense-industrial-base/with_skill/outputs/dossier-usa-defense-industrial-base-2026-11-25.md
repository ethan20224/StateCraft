# Strategy Dossier: Rebuilding America's Arsenal Before It Is Needed

**Principal:** The President and the White House team (Chief of Staff, OMB, the National Security Council), working through the Pentagon (now also styled the Department of War)
**Date:** 25 November 2026
**Horizon:** The lame-duck session (to 3 January 2027), the FY2027 and FY2028 budget cycles, and the January 2029 transition. The design has to survive whoever wins in 2028.
**Version:** 1.0 (first build; assumptions flagged where the principal was unavailable to confirm)

---

## 0. Cabinet Brief

**Bottom line.** The defense industrial base is short of *credible demand*, not money. Firms, workers and allies will not build capacity they expect to sit idle after the next continuing resolution, cancellation or change of administration. The fix is to lock in a decade of predictable orders for a narrow set of things that decide a Pacific war, and to make that commitment bipartisan enough to outlast this presidency.

**Recommended strategy.** Separate an **"arsenal core"** from everything contested. The core is priority munitions and interceptors, submarines, shipyard and ship-repair capacity, the shipbuilding workforce, and the sub-tier suppliers underneath them (solid rocket motors, energetics, seekers, castings and forgings, rare-earth magnets). Fund it in the **base budget and the NDAA**, not in reconciliation. Give it multi-year funding, contractual minimum quantities and termination protection, and protect it from stopgap-budget freezes. Pay for bipartisan ownership with concessions elsewhere: a topline below $1.5 trillion, process guardrails Democrats want, and dropping fights that unite the shipbuilding-state coalition against you, chief among them foreign-built warships. Codify the performance conditions the President has already imposed by executive order, so that they become law rather than one administration's policy.

**Decisions required from the principal.**
1. Whether to give up the $350bn reconciliation ask and the $1.5T topline in exchange for a protected, multi-year arsenal core in base appropriations.
2. Whether to offer process concessions, such as limits on pocket rescissions or nondefense funding levels, as the price of durable defense funding. This is a values-and-power call as much as a tactical one.
3. Whether to slow or re-scope the Trump-class battleship to protect submarine and destroyer throughput and workforce.
4. Whether to drop the fight over building combatants in foreign yards and instead channel allied money into US yards.

**Single biggest risk.** A stopgap budget running into 2027 without "anomalies", or a full-year CR. Under a CR, production cannot rise above last year's rate and new multiyear contracts cannot start. That quietly freezes the munitions framework deals, which depend on annual appropriations.

**Odds line.**
> Current trajectory: Works 0.5 · Passes 0.3 · Delivered 0.4 · Lasts 0.3 → about 2%.
> Recommended strategy: Works 0.7 · Passes 0.6 · Delivered 0.55 · Lasts 0.6 → about 14%.
> **Binding gates: Lasts first, then Delivered.** Passes is less binding for the core than people think, because munitions and submarines have a bipartisan coalition. It becomes binding only when the White House ties the core to contested items.

The headline odds look low because the definition of success (Section 1) is demanding and includes surviving 2029. Partial success, meaning the munitions half substantially fixed by 2030, is far likelier, on the order of a coin flip or better under this strategy.

**This week.** Send appropriators a narrow munitions and submarine anomaly list for the 11 December CR. Tell the NDAA conferees the White House will accept the Senate's broader multiyear-procurement list, a multi-year Defense Production Act reauthorization and the SHIPS maritime provisions, and will not veto over the foreign-yard restriction.

## 1. The Problem, Reframed

### What is actually happening

**Munitions.** The Iran war that began in February 2026 drew down high-end interceptors and long-range strike missiles. CSIS estimates that replacing them will take until roughly the end of the decade, and it argues that the binding constraint is **production time, not money**: lead times of around three years from appropriation to first delivery for some missiles, plus hiring, facilities and supply-chain bottlenecks ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project); [AP via PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/u-s-will-need-years-to-replenish-stockpiles-of-advanced-weapons-used-in-iran-war-new-analysis-finds)). Cheaper bulk munitions are in better shape, but even there money has not equalled output. 155mm shell production was about 36,000 a month in March 2026 against a goal of over 100,000, and a new $469m Army-funded plant in Texas had produced nothing by then ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/)).

There is real progress to build on. In January 2026 the Pentagon and Lockheed agreed a seven-year PAC-3 framework aiming to more than triple output, from 620 delivered in 2025 to 2,000 a year by 2030. The government agreed to reimburse unabsorbed nonrecurring costs if it cuts the deal short ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-lockheed-martin-announce-plans-to-triple-pac-3-production/)). On 31 August, seven-year frameworks followed to triple PAC-3 and quadruple THAAD capacity, but they are explicitly **"subject to annual appropriations"** ([Army Recognition](https://www.armyrecognition.com/news/army-news/2026/u-s-moves-to-triple-pac-3-and-quadruple-thaad-air-defense-missile-production-under-7-year-deals); [Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2026/09/21/the-pentagon-asked-industry-how-fast-it-could-make-more-weapons-the-numbers-remain-private/)).

**Shipbuilding.** CBO's April 2026 testimony is the clearest picture. Destroyers and submarines now take 9–10 years to build, against 5–6 in the 2000s. Virginia-class boats average four years late. Attack-submarine output has averaged 1.1 a year against a buy rate of 2. A third of attack submarines are in or awaiting maintenance, against a 20% goal. Most major yards struggle to hire and keep workers ([CBO testimony](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62375)). The Navy has 291 battle-force ships against a statutory 355, and the shipbuilding budget has doubled over two decades without growing the fleet ([gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/u-s-navy-unveils-65-8-billion-shipbuilding-push-to-launch-trumps-golden-fleet/)). CBO's 19 November analysis puts the new 30-year "Golden Fleet" plan at $53.8bn a year, 41% more in real terms than the last five years' average appropriations, and it would require producing far more tonnage than US yards managed in the past decade ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62884)). The plan adds 15 nuclear-powered battleships that CBO prices at about $23bn for the first and $275bn for all fifteen ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62550)). The Navy's own figure for workforce need is roughly 250,000 hires over a decade just to execute existing plans ([19FortyFive](https://www.19fortyfive.com/2026/07/america-needs-250000-new-shipbuilders-to-build-the-submarines-its-already-paid-for-and-it-cant-find-them/)). Underneath all of it, China builds over half the world's merchant tonnage and the US a fraction of 1%, and Chinese yards build commercial ships and warships side by side ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/threat-chinas-shipbuilding-empire)).

**Chokepoints.** China has suspended its expanded rare-earth and critical-mineral export controls only until **10 January 2027**, under a truce extended on 9 November ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/us/2026/11/09/china-extends-pause-on-rare-earth-export-curbs-for-two-months/)). Domestic solid-rocket-motor supply had consolidated to two firms before the Pentagon's $1bn equity investment in L3Harris's rocket-motor business in January ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-to-invest-1b-in-l3harris-solid-rocket-motor-spin-off/)).

**The budget machine.** FY2026 defense appropriations were enacted only on 3 February 2026, after two lapses and a CR ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48891.html)). FY2027 began on another CR, running to 11 December. It bars new starts, production-rate increases and new multiyear contracts, and appropriators **rejected** munitions-restocking and battleship advance-procurement exceptions ([ClearanceJobs](https://news.clearancejobs.com/2026/10/01/the-pentagon-starts-fiscal-2027-on-a-stopgap-heres-how-it-affects-defense-jobs/)). The FY2027 request of $1.5T splits into $1.15T of base budget and $350bn of reconciliation. The reconciliation tranche holds $113bn for industrial-base capacity and $47bn for munitions ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). One analyst puts lame-duck reconciliation odds at 10%, and the $1.5T request is widely judged dead ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/after-midterms-congress-reckon-defense-funding-ndaa/)).

### The version of the problem we are solving

"The arsenal is a mess" covers at least four distinct problems, each on a different clock:

| Problem | Clock | Binding constraint | Can this administration fix it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-end munitions and interceptors | 3–5 years | Demand credibility (CRs, annual appropriations); sub-tier suppliers; lead times | Yes, substantially, by 2030 |
| Submarines and surface combatants | 10–20 years | Workforce, yard capacity, design churn, a vanished commercial base | No. It can set the trajectory and lock it in. |
| Ship repair and maintenance (the hidden fleet) | 2–6 years | Public-yard capacity, dry docks, workforce | Partly. This is the fastest way to add effective ships. |
| Chinese chokepoints in sub-tier inputs | 1–5 years | Processing capacity outside China; price risk | Partly; the January 2027 deadline forces action |

**We attack the first, third and fourth hardest, and put the second on a stable trajectory.** The reasoning: a war in the Western Pacific in the next five years would be fought largely with the fleet that already exists, so for wartime capability the shipbuilding levers that matter are repair throughput and keeping submarines on schedule. Munitions, by contrast, are consumed fast, can be expanded within a presidential term, and their visible depth is itself a deterrent. Long-war shipbuilding capacity matters most for *deterrence*: Beijing has to believe America could sustain a war, which it judges by watching whether US capacity is growing.

### Root-cause tree (abridged)

- **Erratic demand signal** → firms don't invest in capacity; suppliers exit; workers aren't hired
  - Annual appropriations plus habitual CRs that legally freeze rates and new starts
  - Programme churn (frigate cost growth and redesign, battleship pivot, stop-start) — CBO explicitly warns against returning to the Zumwalt-era pattern
  - Whiplash between administrations
- **Thin, consolidated supply base** → single points of failure
  - The post-1993 consolidation ("the Last Supper") shrank roughly 51 prime contractors to 5; sub-tiers hollowed out further
  - Commercial shipbuilding collapse removed the civilian base that once absorbed surge
- **Workforce** → yards can't hire or keep skilled trades at current wages and conditions
- **Buyer behaviour** → slow contracting, requirements creep, cost-plus incentives, payouts to shareholders over capacity investment (the problem the January executive order targets)
- **Foreign chokepoints** → Chinese control of rare-earth processing and some energetics inputs

### Who benefits from the status quo

Shareholders of primes rewarded for buybacks over capex. Appropriators who prefer annual control to multi-year commitments. Services that protect favoured exquisite programmes over munitions depth. Beijing, which benefits from US budget dysfunction more than from any single programme choice.

### Cost of doing nothing

A multi-year window in which US munitions depth is thin, submarine availability stays low and repair queues stay long. Adversaries notice, and deterrence erodes without any visible event. The status quo is not stable. Each CR and cancellation teaches suppliers to wait, so it gets worse over time.

### Definition of success (by end of FY2030)

1. Priority munitions and interceptors are delivered at their contracted surge rates (for example, PAC-3 at or near 2,000 a year), with minimum quantities funded in law every year through FY2033.
2. Attack-submarine deliveries are sustained above 1.5 a year and trending to 2, the submarine maintenance backlog is down toward the 20% goal, and Columbia stays on its recovered schedule.
3. Defense-grade rare-earth magnets and the priority energetics have a supply chain that does not depend on Chinese licences.
4. The funding architecture survives the January 2029 transition intact.

## 2. The Seat and the Constraints

**Who has which powers.** The President can direct the Pentagon, use Defense Production Act authorities, sign framework deals, take equity stakes through industrial-base funds, impose contract conditions (as in the 7 January executive order on buybacks and executive pay, [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/01/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-prioritizes-the-warfighter-in-defense-contracting)) and negotiate with allies. The President cannot appropriate money, grant multi-year funding, exempt programmes from CR rules or make any of this durable. Only Congress can.

**The clock.**
- **Now to 11 December:** the CR expires. DPA authorities also run only to 11 December after short extensions ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/powerful-cold-war-era-tool-trump-could-use-ai-emergency-set-expire-december); [CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-02-12_IN12484_9826aa0a51bac9e807aa8db4bb4dd54c8c1a031f.html)).
- **December:** FY2027 NDAA conference. The "Big Four" were due to begin negotiating in mid-November ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)).
- **3 January 2027:** the new Congress convenes.
- **10 January 2027:** China's suspension of mineral export controls expires.
- **Early February:** the FY2028 budget request. The industry responses to the Deputy Secretary's 21-day acceleration request were meant to inform it ([Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2026/09/21/the-pentagon-asked-industry-how-fast-it-could-make-more-weapons-the-numbers-remain-private/)).
- **Early to mid 2027:** the debt limit could be reached, per CSIS ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/fy-2027-defense-funding-potential-post-midterm-outcomes)).
- **November 2028 / January 2029:** the durability test.

**The new Congress.** As of Nov 25, 10:33am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with a total of 56 Senate seats and Republicans with a total of 44. The label "Control reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. As of Nov 25, 10:34am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with 259 House seats won and Republicans with 173. The label "Majority reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. Three House seats are counted as won for no party in the AP results I have, and those results don't say why or which way they will go. These numbers may have changed since. **Going by AP's count of those seats, this dossier plans for a Republican President working with Democratic majorities in both chambers from 3 January.** Two consequences follow. Party-line reconciliation is effectively available only in the lame duck. And everything durable must be bipartisan, which is an asset for the Lasts gate if the White House uses it.

**Political capital.** Lower than in 2025. The Iran war, the topline and the "Department of War" renaming are live disputes in the NDAA. The industrial base is one of the few areas where the President's goals and the incoming committee leaders' goals overlap.

**Fiscal room.** Thin. Debt held by the public is about 100% of GDP. Net interest was $970bn in FY2025 and CBO projects $2.1T by 2036 ([CRFB on CBO](https://www.crfb.org/papers/cbos-february-2026-budget-and-economic-outlook)). A $1.5T topline invites a deficit fight the arsenal core does not need.

**Legal limits.** CR rules (no new starts, no rate increases, no new multiyear contracts without anomalies). Both chambers' NDAA texts restrict buying battle-force ships from foreign yards ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook); [Korea Times on the OMB statement](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/foreignaffairs/others/20260805/white-house-notes-its-proposal-to-build-2-combat-2-noncombat-vessels-in-foreign-shipyards-in-memo)). Appropriations clauses limit how far equity stakes and direct-to-supplier deals can scale without Congress.

## 3. What History Teaches

**The real precedent is 1936, not 1942.** The Liberty ship miracle (2,710 hulls) is usually told as wartime improvisation. It rested on the Merchant Marine Act of 1936 and the Maritime Commission's long-range programme of steady annual orders, which rebuilt yards, suppliers and skills *before* the war. Between 1939 and 1941, British and French orders and government-financed plants expanded capacity before Pearl Harbor. **What transfers:** surge capacity is built in peacetime by predictable demand. **What doesn't:** total mobilisation, unlimited money and a large commercial shipbuilding base that no longer exists. Plan for a decade of steady orders, not a mobilisation fantasy.

**The 1993 consolidation.** The "Last Supper" told industry to consolidate. It bought efficiency at the expense of surge capacity and competition. **Lesson:** sole-source chokepoints are a policy choice, and so is reversing them through second sources, new entrants and direct sub-tier investment.

**Ukraine-driven ramps since 2022.** 155mm output roughly 2.5x'd but fell far short of the goal, and the flagship new plant produced nothing for years ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/)). **Lesson:** capacity announcements are not deliveries. Measure output.

**CHIPS Act (2022–).** Subsidies were necessary but not sufficient. Workforce, permitting and suppliers were the binding constraints. **Lesson:** fund the ecosystem, not just the primes.

**Inflation Reduction Act (2022–25).** When Congress rolled much of it back, local-factory credits survived and diffuse consumer credits did not. **Lesson:** benefits rooted in local jobs build cross-party defenders. Spread the arsenal geographically and visibly.

**South Korea's industrialisation.** State support was conditional on measurable performance. **Lesson:** the executive order's performance conditions are right in principle. Codify them so they discipline rather than punish arbitrarily.

**Thatcher and the coal stockpiles (1981–85).** She backed off, prepared for three years and then won. **Lesson:** don't let the 10 January mineral deadline be the moment you discover you have no stockpile or alternative supply.

**Navy programme churn.** CBO warns that returning to the Zumwalt-era stop-start pattern would be costly ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62375)). **Lesson:** design stability is a capacity policy. The battleship has to be judged by what it does to the yards, not only by what it adds at sea.

**Concentrated and diffuse.** The benefits of the arsenal core are concentrated in specific states and districts, and the costs are diffuse (taxpayers). That is classic client politics, and it is why munitions and submarines have historically passed easily. The danger is not opposition to the core. It is **bundling** the core with things that provoke concentrated, intense opposition: the Iran war, the topline, Golden Dome, the battleship, the renaming and foreign-built warships.

**Veto points.** Appropriations subcommittees (on CR anomalies and multi-year money); the NDAA conference; the Senate's 60-vote threshold; the presidential veto (a risk if the White House vetoes the NDAA over Iran language or the foreign-yard ban); courts (equity deals, DPA use); and Beijing's export-licence desk.

**The coalition path.** Shipbuilding and munitions-state members of both parties, plus Armed Services leaders of both parties, plus appropriators bought with process and nondefense concessions. That coalition already exists. The White House's job is to stop dividing it.

## 5. Options Considered

| Option | Description | Works | Passes | Delivered | Lasts | ≈ Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Topline surge** | Fight for $1.5T including $350bn reconciliation; industrial-base money mostly in reconciliation | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.4 | 0.3 | <1% |
| **B. Arsenal-core bargain (recommended)** | Protected multi-year core in base budget and NDAA; bipartisan; traded for topline, process and foreign-yard concessions | 0.7 | 0.6 | 0.55 | 0.6 | ~14% |
| **C. Executive command economy** | DPA, executive orders, equity stakes, contractor penalties, with minimal reliance on Congress | 0.5 | 0.8 | 0.4 | 0.15 | ~2% |
| **D. Allied arsenal** | Buy combatants from Korean and Japanese yards; lean on allied co-production | 0.6 | 0.25 | 0.6 | 0.4 | ~4% |

**Why B wins.** A puts the money in the vehicle least likely to pass and invites a deficit war. C is fast but fragile: DPA authority is on a short leash, equity deals face subpoena-backed oversight in the new Congress, and a successor can reverse everything. D has real merit on speed but runs straight into a provision both chambers have written. **B absorbs the best parts of C and D.** It keeps direct-to-supplier deals and performance conditions, but in statute. It keeps allied capital, but invested in US yards, with foreign construction limited to auxiliaries if Congress agrees. Its weak gate is Delivered, which no amount of politics fixes. Hence the heavy delivery architecture in Section 8.

## 6. The Strategy

**Theory of change.** *If* the government makes a credible, legally protected, decade-long commitment to buy specific quantities of a narrow set of priority munitions, submarines and repair capacity, and pays for sub-tier and workforce capacity directly, *then* primes, suppliers, workers and allies will invest in capacity ahead of orders, *because* the main reason they don't today is the rational expectation that demand will be frozen, cut or reinvented before their investment pays back.

**Design features.**

1. **The arsenal core, defined narrowly.** A short statutory list: priority long-range strike and anti-ship munitions; air- and missile-defense interceptors; submarines (Columbia first, Virginia second); public and private ship-repair capacity; the shipyard workforce; and named sub-tier categories (solid rocket motors, energetics, seekers, castings and forgings, rare-earth magnets). Narrowness is what makes it defensible. Every addition makes it more like the topline fight.

2. **Money in the base budget, multi-year.** Move the industrial-base and munitions money out of reconciliation and into base appropriations. The Pentagon comptroller has already said the department would shift reconciliation priorities into discretionary funding if needed ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/fy-2027-defense-funding-potential-post-midterm-outcomes)). Seek multi-year availability for capacity investments (tooling, facilities, second sources) and multiyear procurement for the priority munitions. Accept the Senate's 19-munition list over the House's 13 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)).

3. **CR-proofing.** Two layers. Near term: a narrow, pre-negotiated anomaly package for every CR, covering rate increases and multiyear starts for core lines only. Durable: work with appropriators on a standing exception for designated core lines. Appropriators will resist giving up their own leverage, so offer them the designation power and reporting requirements. If they refuse, the fallback is multi-year appropriations, which survive a CR anyway.

4. **Codify the framework model.** The PAC-3 deal's features (seven-year horizon, supplier subcontracts, inflation provisions, reimbursement of unabsorbed nonrecurring costs if the government walks away) should become a statutory template for core munitions. **Termination liability is the durability mechanism.** A future administration can still cancel, but it has to pay to do so, and that changes its calculation.

5. **Conditions with teeth, in law.** Write a calibrated version of the executive order's performance conditions into the NDAA: limits on buybacks and dividends during underperformance, and incentive pay tied to delivery. The Senate bill already bars defense stock buybacks and dividends. Calibrate it to performance rather than a blanket ban, so it disciplines without driving capital away. This converts a presidential policy into a bipartisan law and gives Democrats an oversight win they want.

6. **Ships: stability over ambition.** Prioritise submarine throughput, repair capacity and design stability, which is CBO's own advice ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62375)). Re-scope the battleship. Keep it in design and studies, and keep the Golden Fleet brand. But defer the procurement profile until submarine output shows sustained recovery, because CBO finds it more than doubles the large-surface-combatant tonnage yards must produce by 2035 ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62550)). Push distributed and modular construction (the Navy's goal is to raise distributed work from about 10% to 50%; [gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/u-s-navy-unveils-65-8-billion-shipbuilding-push-to-launch-trumps-golden-fleet/)).

7. **Allied capital into US yards.** Treat Korea's $150bn MASGA pledge and Hanwha's Philadelphia yard as the model. Hanwha's first Navy order, a medium landing ship, came on 1 November ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/companies/20261101/hanwha-bags-1st-us-navy-shipbuilding-order)). Drop the demand to build combatants abroad. Ask instead for a narrow authority for foreign-built *auxiliaries* and for allied repair of US ships in allied yards, both of which the shipbuilding-state coalition can live with. Expand munitions co-production with Japan and Australia, but write allocation rules so allied deliveries add capacity rather than compete with US restocking (CSIS flags this friction; [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project)).

8. **Workforce as infrastructure.** Fund wages, training and housing near yards. Allow wage pass-through in shipbuilding contracts. Scale registered apprenticeships and community-college pipelines through existing Labor Department and Navy channels rather than new agencies. The biggest delivery risk is people, and it has a natural Democratic constituency in unions, which matters for durability.

9. **Chokepoints before the deadline.** Before 10 January: stockpile purchases for the National Defense Stockpile, price floors and offtake agreements (the 2025 MP Materials structure is the template), and allied processing deals. Then decide deliberately how much export-control leverage to trade for mineral access. That is a strategic values call (Section 14).

10. **Reauthorize the DPA for five years in the NDAA** rather than living on short extensions.

## 7. The Campaign

### Phase 1: Lame duck (now to 3 January). *Bank the authorities.*
- **CR (by 11 December):** spend capital here first. Ask only for core anomalies (munitions rate increases and multiyear starts; submarine and repair lines). Drop the battleship advance-procurement anomaly. It was already rejected once and it poisons the rest.
- **NDAA:** accept the Senate's multiyear list; accept the SHIPS maritime package the House adopted ([Heritage](https://www.heritage.org/node/25160397)); push a five-year DPA reauthorization; accept a calibrated buyback provision. **Do not veto the NDAA** over the foreign-yard restriction or Iran reporting language. A veto would hand the new Congress a fight on its own terms and delay the authorities the arsenal needs.
- **Reconciliation:** don't chase $350bn. Precedents took 42–85 days and only about 20 session days remain ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/fy-2027-defense-funding-potential-post-midterm-outcomes)). If the $60bn package moves anyway, use it for one-time capital (tooling, plants, second sources), not recurring buys that will need base funding next year.

### Phase 2: The new Congress (January to March 2027). *Make it bipartisan.*
- In the first weeks, the President invites the Armed Services leaders of both parties and the shipbuilding and munitions-state senators to co-launch an "Arsenal" bill: the multi-year core, the framework template, workforce and chokepoints. Let Democrats co-author and name it. Credit-sharing is the price of durability.
- The FY2028 request presents the core as a separate, protected account in the base budget, with a five-year funding profile.
- Brief Congress, on a classified basis, on the industry responses to the 21-day request. Demand-side transparency buys appropriators' trust.
- Keep the arsenal core out of the debt-limit fight. Defense hostages in a debt fight teach industry the opposite of what you want it to learn.

### Phase 3: Delivery and lock-in (2027–2028).
- Monthly stocktakes. Publish an unclassified scorecard of deliveries against trajectories.
- Award second sources for every sole-source sub-tier chokepoint.
- Sign multi-year co-production agreements with allies, so foreign partners become stakeholders in continuity.
- Before the 2028 election, make sure every core line has multiyear contracts with termination liability running past 2030.

### Narrative by audience
- **Republicans:** peace through strength; ending profiteering by underperforming contractors; America First manufacturing.
- **Democrats:** good union jobs in their states; accountability for contractors; deterrence that makes war less likely; fiscal discipline through a lower topline.
- **Industry:** a decade of predictable demand in exchange for real capital investment and delivery.
- **Allies:** invest in America and share in the orders.
- **Beijing (implicitly):** the US arsenal is growing every year regardless of who governs.

**Avoid** the "Department of War" branding and Iran-war framing in the arsenal bill. Both are identity-coded and turn a client-politics issue into a team sport.

### Handling the losers
- **Primes losing payout freedom:** offset with termination protection and shared savings. Conditions apply only during underperformance.
- **Services losing pet programmes:** the battleship is deferred, not cancelled, and keeps its brand and design funding.
- **Appropriators losing annual leverage:** they keep designation power and reporting requirements and get process guardrails.
- **Fiscal hawks:** a lower topline than requested.

## 8. Delivery Architecture

**Owner.** One accountable official at the Pentagon: the Deputy Secretary, who already runs the munitions acceleration push. A small **Arsenal Delivery Unit** (10–15 people) sits jointly at the NSC and OMB. It tracks five to eight core lines against delivery trajectories and holds a monthly stocktake chaired by the Chief of Staff, with a quarterly session for the President. Few priorities, data, routine and leader attention: the UK Delivery Unit model.

**Agencies.**
- Pentagon acquisition (portfolio acquisition executives, newly empowered by the FY2026 NDAA; [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/)) run the frameworks and multiyear contracts.
- The Navy runs the submarine and repair lines and the distributed-construction push.
- The Industrial Base Analysis and Sustainment programme and the Office of Strategic Capital run direct-to-supplier investments.
- Commerce and the Defense Logistics Agency run critical minerals and the stockpile.
- Labor runs apprenticeships.
- State coordinates allied co-production and MASGA.

**Capacity gaps and fixes.**
- **Contracting speed.** Measure appropriation-to-contract time and target under 90 days for core lines.
- **Cost estimating.** Use reference-class forecasting. CBO's estimates run about 18% above the Navy's on the shipbuilding plan ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62884)), so budget to CBO.
- **Sub-tier visibility.** The FY2026 NDAA requires mapping supply-chain vulnerabilities and qualifying second sources. Fund that work and publish a classified chokepoint register.

**Data.** One dashboard shared with the Armed Services leaders of both parties. Shared data creates shared ownership.

**Financing.** Base appropriations with multi-year availability for capacity. Multiyear procurement for core munitions and submarines. Termination liability funded up front. Equity stakes only with transparent terms, return expectations and exit rules. Holland & Knight expects heavy congressional oversight of them in the new Congress, so make them defensible before the subpoena arrives ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)).

**Pilot to scale.** The PAC-3 and THAAD frameworks are the pilot. Scale the template to other core munitions only after the first annual delivery data shows the model works, since capacity is not output.

## 9. The Wargame

| Actor | Their best move | Our counter | Two moves on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Democratic appropriators (DeLauro, Murray) | A short CR into the new year with no anomalies, to maximise leverage on nondefense and pocket rescissions | Offer a narrow core anomaly package that is cheap and bipartisan, and pair it with a process concession | They take the concession but demand clawback of reconciliation funds. Accept moving reconciliation money into base, where they control it. That is what we want anyway. |
| Senior House Armed Services Democrat (Adam Smith) | Aggressive oversight: Iran, equity deals, Golden Dome | Pre-emptive transparency on equity deals; separate the arsenal bill from contested items | Oversight continues on Iran, but the arsenal bill becomes his signature achievement too |
| Primes | Lobby against calibrated buyback limits and right-to-repair IP provisions; slow-walk capex | Termination protection and shared savings in exchange for capex commitments; remediation under the executive order; second sources | Some primes comply. Open a second source on the laggard's line as an example, which disciplines the rest. |
| Fiscal hawks | Demand offsets; hold out on the CR | A lower topline; the core as the protected priority within it | They accept the core because it is smaller than the request |
| China | Re-impose mineral controls at the 10 January expiry or use them as leverage in talks; read US munitions depth as a window | Stockpile and allied supply before the deadline; signal that arsenal funding is bipartisan and multi-year | Beijing's leverage falls as alternatives come online; its incentive to escalate on minerals falls with it |
| Allies | Hedge if Congress blocks their yards; demand returns on MASGA | Channel investment into US yards; auxiliaries and repair as early wins | Allied capital becomes a pro-continuity constituency inside US politics |
| A future administration (2029) | Cancel or reprioritise | Termination liability, statute, geographic spread, union and allied stakeholders | Cancellation becomes expensive and politically costly in dozens of states |

**Second- and third-order effects.**
- *Three months:* anomalies keep the frameworks alive; suppliers sign subcontracts; hiring begins.
- *Three years:* munitions output visibly climbs; submarine output improves slowly; wage pressure in yard towns; possible inflation in defense-specific inputs.
- *Ten years:* a deeper, more competitive supply base, or, if the 2029 transition breaks it, another boom-bust cycle that teaches industry never to invest ahead of orders again.

**Market reaction.** Defense equities like multi-year demand and dislike payout limits. Expect lobbying, not capital flight, if the conditions are calibrated to performance.

## 10. Pre-Mortem and Red Team

**Failure story 1: the freeze.** The lame duck ends with a CR into March without anomalies. It is followed by a full-year CR as the debt-limit fight consumes 2027. The framework deals sit unfunded at prior-year rates, suppliers delay hiring, and by 2028 the "tripling" exists only in press releases. *Probability: meaningful.* *Prevention:* make the anomaly package the first and highest-priority ask, and pay for it.

**Failure story 2: money without output.** Funding flows, but sub-tier bottlenecks (rocket motors, energetics, castings) and workforce shortages cap output, repeating the 155mm story. *Prevention:* direct sub-tier investment, second sources, delivery-based metrics and kill criteria for investments that do not convert to output.

**Failure story 3: identity capture.** The arsenal becomes coded as "Trump's war machine" through association with the Iran war, Golden Dome and the battleship. The new Congress cuts the topline and the core goes with it. *Prevention:* separate the bill, co-author it with Democrats, use local-jobs framing and keep contested items out.

**The opposition's strongest case.** "This is a contractor bailout dressed as national security. The same primes that took buybacks while lines went cold now get seven-year guarantees and termination payments. Meanwhile the President wants $1.5 trillion, a battleship fleet and a war Congress never authorised." *Answer:* the performance conditions, the calibrated buyback limits and the lower topline are designed precisely to meet this. The strongest part of the critique, unconditional guarantees to primes, is a real risk, and the conditions must be real.

**Hirschman's three attacks.**
- *Perversity* ("it will entrench the primes and raise prices"): direct-to-supplier deals, second sources and new entrants reduce dependence on primes. Shared-savings clauses capture learning-curve gains for taxpayers.
- *Futility* ("China builds 200 times more ships; we can't catch up"): we don't need to match tonnage. We need munitions depth, submarine output, repair capacity and allied yards. Korea and Japan are among the world's largest shipbuilders, so the relevant comparison is the allied base, not the US alone.
- *Jeopardy* ("this crowds out domestic needs and deepens the debt"): the trade-off is real, which is why the recommendation is a protected core within a lower topline, not $1.5T.

## 11. Durability

**Lock-in mechanisms.**
1. Statute (NDAA authorities, a five-year DPA reauthorization, the framework template, calibrated conditions).
2. Multiyear contracts with termination liability running past 2030.
3. Multi-year appropriations availability for capacity investment.
4. Geographic spread across many states and districts in both parties.
5. Allied co-investment and co-production agreements.
6. Workforce pipelines with union stakeholders.
7. A shared bipartisan dashboard.

**Constituencies created.** Plant towns, yard towns, unions, allied investors, new-entrant firms, and the members of Congress who co-authored and named the bill.

**Surviving a change of government.** A successor of either party inherits a law it helped write, contracts that are expensive to break and constituencies that will punish cancellation. The executive-order-only path offers none of this. That is the core reason to accept the political costs of the bargain.

## 12. The Dashboard

| Indicator | Type | Target | Tripwire → pre-agreed response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Months core lines spend frozen under a CR | Leading | Zero | Any CR without core anomalies → emergency request for a standalone anomaly bill; reprogramming; DPA Title III bridge funding |
| Funded minimum quantities vs framework schedules | Leading | 100% each year | Below 90% → Delivery Unit escalation to the Chief of Staff; FY+1 request corrects it |
| Monthly deliveries vs trajectory, per core munition | Lagging | On trajectory to 2030 targets | Two quarters >20% behind → remediation under the executive order; second-source award |
| Appropriation-to-contract time | Leading | Under 90 days | Over 120 days → acquisition review of the portfolio |
| Sub-tier lead times (rocket motors, energetics, seekers, castings) | Leading | Falling year on year | Rising two quarters running → direct-to-supplier investment |
| Yard net hiring and first-year attrition | Leading | Net positive hiring; attrition below 10% (CBO: Electric Boat and Bath now below 10%, Ingalls about 15%) | Attrition above 15% → wage pass-through review; housing and training funds |
| Virginia-class deliveries per year | Lagging | 1.5 by 2028, trending to 2 | Below 1.2 in 2028 → defer battleship profile further |
| Submarines in or awaiting maintenance | Lagging | Toward 20% (from about 33%) | No improvement by 2028 → surge public-yard and allied repair |
| Rare-earth magnet supply independent of Chinese licences | Leading | Rising share each year | Controls re-imposed → stockpile release; allied supply activation |
| Allied capital invested in US yards and plants | Leading | Rising | Stalls → review the MASGA terms |

**Goodhart warning.** Count deliveries, not "capacity". Capacity claims are the easiest number to game, and CSIS notes that capacity is not output.

**Review cadence.** Monthly Delivery Unit stocktake; quarterly presidential review; annual public scorecard to Congress.

**Kill criteria.** Any direct equity or capacity investment that shows no measurable output gain within 24 months gets exited or restructured. The battleship procurement profile stays deferred until submarine deliveries sustain 1.5 a year.

## 14. Confidence and Open Questions

**Known.** CR legal restrictions; CBO's shipbuilding numbers; the existence and terms of the PAC-3 and THAAD frameworks; differences between the House and Senate NDAA texts; the January 2027 mineral deadline; the CR's 11 December expiry.

**Assumed.** That senior Democratic appropriators will trade core anomalies and multi-year money for process and nondefense concessions. That Democratic Armed Services leaders will co-own a narrow arsenal bill despite their oversight agenda. That primes will accept calibrated conditions in exchange for termination protection. That China keeps mineral leverage as a bargaining chip rather than escalating sharply.

**Judgment.** That Lasts and Delivered, not Passes, are the binding gates for the core. That the battleship is a net negative for near-term capacity. That foreign-built combatants are not worth the political cost this Congress.

**What would change the recommendation.**
- If Congress offered multi-year protected funding without concessions, spend the saved capital elsewhere.
- If a Taiwan Strait crisis erupted, shift to crisis mode: emergency supplemental, DPA priority ratings, allied surge.
- If submarine output recovered faster than expected, the battleship deferral could ease.
- If the 21-day industry responses showed money (not time) was the binding constraint, a larger topline would deserve more weight.

**Values calls for the principal.**
1. **Process concessions.** Trading limits on pocket rescissions for durable defense funding is a decision about presidential power, not only tactics.
2. **Battleship vs submarines.** Visible new capability against near-term throughput.
3. **Export-control leverage vs mineral access.** The public is split: 48% support and 44% oppose a hypothetical deal easing tech restrictions for rare earths ([Reagan Institute](https://www.reaganfoundation.org/cms/assets/1782414649-reagan-institute-summer-survey-2026-executive-memo.pdf)).
4. **Topline vs deficit.** How much fiscal risk to accept for defense growth.

## 15. The Statecraft Lesson

Capacity follows credible demand, not money. The Liberty ship miracle is remembered as wartime genius, but it rested on the 1936 Merchant Marine Act's decade of steady orders and on foreign orders that expanded plants before Pearl Harbor. When the moment came, there were yards, suppliers and skilled workers to scale. The transferable principle is that in any system with long lead times, from arsenals to power grids to chip fabs, the state's most valuable asset is believable commitment: commitment that survives budget cycles, elections and its own authors. Policies built on executive will alone are bets on one person staying in power. Policies built into law, contracts and constituencies are bets on the system. **The question to sit with:** what would you give up to make the arsenal bipartisan, and are you sure the thing you want to keep matters more than the decade of predictable demand that giving it up would buy?
