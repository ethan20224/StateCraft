**Short answer:** treat this as a credibility problem, not a money problem. Firms, workers and allies only build capacity for demand they believe will still exist in 2030. Washington currently signals the opposite. Stopgap budgets legally freeze production increases. Flagship programmes get cancelled or reinvented every few years. And the biggest pot of industrial-base money in this year's request sits in the legislative vehicle least likely to pass.

The strategy that works is narrower and less glamorous than a $1.5 trillion topline. Carve out a protected, multi-year, bipartisan **"arsenal core"**: priority munitions and interceptors, submarines, ship-repair capacity, the shipyard workforce, and the sub-tier suppliers underneath them. Then buy its durability by conceding on almost everything else.

### First: the political ground just moved

As of Nov 25, 10:33am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with a total of 56 Senate seats and Republicans with a total of 44. The label "Control reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. As of Nov 25, 10:34am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with 259 House seats won and Republicans with 173. The label "Majority reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. Three House seats are counted as won for no party in the AP results I have, and those results don't say why or which way they'll go. All of these numbers may have changed since.

Going by AP's count of those seats, I'm building this for a Republican President who has a lame-duck session until 3 January and then faces Democratic majorities in both chambers. That has two consequences:

- **The party-line route is closing.** The administration's request is $1.15T in base budget plus $350bn through reconciliation. The reconciliation piece holds $113bn for industrial-base capacity and $47bn for munitions ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). One analyst puts lame-duck reconciliation odds at about 10%, and the $1.5T request is widely judged dead ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/after-midterms-congress-reckon-defense-funding-ndaa/)).
- **This helps durability more than it hurts.** Anything that lasts past 2029 was always going to need bipartisan law.

### Where your framing is right, and where it misleads

You're right that the base is badly short. On ships, CBO testified in April that destroyers and submarines now take 9–10 years to build, against 5–6 in the 2000s. Attack-submarine output has averaged about 1.1 a year against a target of 2, and a third of attack subs are stuck in or waiting for maintenance ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62375)). On munitions, the Iran war drew down high-end interceptors and long-range missiles, and CSIS estimates replacing them runs to roughly the end of the decade ([AP via PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/u-s-will-need-years-to-replenish-stockpiles-of-advanced-weapons-used-in-iran-war-new-analysis-finds)).

But "a mess" hides three things that would change what you do.

**First, it is four problems on four clocks, not one.**

| Problem | Realistic fix time | Can this administration fix it? |
|---|---|---|
| High-end munitions | 3–5 years | Yes, substantially |
| Shipbuilding | 15–20 years | No. It can only set the trajectory and lock it in. |
| Ship repair | 2–6 years | Partly. It's the quickest way to add effective ships. |
| Chinese chokepoints in minerals and sub-tier parts | 1–5 years | Partly, and there's a hard deadline: China's suspension of its mineral export controls expires on **10 January** ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/us/2026/11/09/china-extends-pause-on-rare-earth-export-curbs-for-two-months/)) |

A serious strategy attacks munitions, repair and chokepoints hardest, and puts shipbuilding on a stable path.

**Second, "long war" is the wrong lens for ships.** A Pacific war in the next five years would be fought largely with the fleet that already exists. No shipbuilding surge changes that. Long-war capacity matters for *deterrence*, because Beijing judges whether America could sustain a war partly by watching whether US capacity is growing. So in the near term, the shipbuilding levers that matter are keeping submarines on schedule and getting ships out of repair queues, not laying down new hull types.

**Third, it is less a money problem than a buyer problem.** CSIS's own conclusion is that the binding constraint on munitions is production time, not funding ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project)). The cautionary tale is 155mm shells. After four years of urgency and billions of dollars, output was about 36,000 a month in March against a goal of over 100,000, and a new $469m Army-funded plant in Texas had produced nothing ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/)).

Meanwhile the government keeps undercutting its own demand signal. The stopgap funding the Pentagon right now bars production-rate increases and new multiyear contracts. Appropriators *rejected* exceptions for restocking interceptors and missiles ([ClearanceJobs](https://news.clearancejobs.com/2026/10/01/the-pentagon-starts-fiscal-2027-on-a-stopgap-heres-how-it-affects-defense-jobs/)). And the new seven-year deals to triple PAC-3 and quadruple THAAD capacity are explicitly "subject to annual appropriations" ([Army Recognition](https://www.armyrecognition.com/news/army-news/2026/u-s-moves-to-triple-pac-3-and-quadruple-thaad-air-defense-missile-production-under-7-year-deals)). You can't ask a supplier to hire for 2030 while telling it, every December, that next year's order might be frozen at this year's level.

### The binding gate

Any reform succeeds only if it works, passes, gets delivered, and lasts. Here, the binding gate is **Lasts**, with **Delivered** close behind.

**Passes** is less binding for the core than it looks. Munitions plants and shipyards are spread across many states with members of both parties: Connecticut, Rhode Island, Virginia, Maine, Mississippi, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin for ships; Arizona, Arkansas, Texas, Alabama and others for munitions. That is classic client politics, and it is why these programmes usually pass easily. Passes becomes binding only when the White House bundles the core with contested items: the Iran war, the topline, Golden Dome, the battleship, the "Department of War" renaming and foreign-built warships.

> **Odds line, current trajectory:** Works 0.5 · Passes 0.3 · Delivered 0.4 · Lasts 0.3 → about 2%.
> **Odds line, recommended strategy:** Works 0.7 · Passes 0.6 · Delivered 0.55 · Lasts 0.6 → about 14%.

These numbers are my judgment, not measurement, and the gates aren't independent. "Success" here is demanding:

- priority munitions delivered at contracted surge rates by 2030, with minimum buys funded in law through 2033;
- submarine deliveries above 1.5 a year and climbing;
- defense-grade rare-earth magnets that don't depend on Chinese licences;
- all of it surviving the January 2029 handover.

Partial success, meaning the munitions half substantially fixed, is closer to a coin flip under this plan.

### The strategy: five moves, in order

**1. Spend the lame duck banking authorities, not chasing money.** The stopgap expires 11 December, and so do the Defense Production Act's authorities after a string of short extensions ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/powerful-cold-war-era-tool-trump-could-use-ai-emergency-set-expire-december)).

- **Anomalies.** The single highest-value ask is a narrow package of exceptions in the next stopgap. These are called "anomalies", and these would let core munitions and submarine lines raise rates and start multiyear contracts. Drop the battleship advance-procurement request that was already rejected once; it poisons the rest.
- **The NDAA.** Accept the Senate's list of 19 munitions for multiyear buys over the House's 13. Accept the SHIPS for America maritime package the House folded in ([Heritage](https://www.heritage.org/node/25160397)). Push a five-year DPA reauthorization. Accept a buyback limit tied to performance.
- **Reconciliation.** Don't chase $350bn. Past reconciliation bills took 42–85 days from budget resolution to enactment, and roughly 20 session days remain ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/fy-2027-defense-funding-potential-post-midterm-outcomes)).

**2. Move the industrial-base money into the base budget and make it multi-year.** This is the bargain with the new Congress. The Pentagon's comptroller has already said reconciliation priorities would shift into regular appropriations if needed. Appropriators want that money where they control it. That aligns with what you want: protected multi-year funding for capacity, plus multiyear procurement for core lines.

The price is a topline below $1.5T, plus whatever Democratic appropriators need on nondefense funding and limits on pocket rescissions ([Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/print/v2/content/1140457/the-midterms-reset-the-fy27-appropriations-endgame.pdf)). That last item is a real question about presidential power, not just tactics. It's the President's call.

**3. Turn the munitions deals into law, with teeth on both sides.** The PAC-3 deal has the right structure: seven years, supplier subcontracts, and the government reimbursing unabsorbed costs if it walks away. The deal aims to take output from 620 missiles in 2025 to 2,000 a year by 2030 ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-lockheed-martin-announce-plans-to-triple-pac-3-production/)). Make that structure a statutory template for the core. **Termination liability is the durability mechanism.** A future administration can still cancel, but it has to pay to do so.

Pair it with the President's January executive order limiting buybacks and executive pay at underperforming contractors ([White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/01/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-prioritizes-the-warfighter-in-defense-contracting)), written into the NDAA. That turns one administration's policy into bipartisan law, and it gives progressives the anti-profiteering win they want. Keep investing below the primes: the $1bn stake in L3Harris's rocket-motor business is the template ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-to-invest-1b-in-l3harris-solid-rocket-motor-spin-off/)). Exit any investment that doesn't raise output within two years.

**4. For ships, choose stability over ambition.** CBO's advice is design stability and steady multiyear buys. Its new estimate puts the 30-year "Golden Fleet" plan at $53.8bn a year, 41% above recent appropriations, and requiring far more tonnage than US yards have managed in a decade ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62884)). Put submarines, repair and the workforce first. The Navy's own figure is roughly 250,000 hires over ten years ([19FortyFive](https://www.19fortyfive.com/2026/07/america-needs-250000-new-shipbuilders-to-build-the-submarines-its-already-paid-for-and-it-cant-find-them/)).

Bring allied capital *into* US yards. Hanwha's Philadelphia yard won its first Navy order on 1 November under Korea's $150bn shipbuilding pledge ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/companies/20261101/hanwha-bags-1st-us-navy-shipbuilding-order)). Use allied yards for auxiliaries and repair rather than combatants.

**5. Make it stick before 2029.** Put the core in a co-authored bill the Democratic Armed Services leaders can name and own. Credit-sharing is the price of durability. Spread work visibly across districts. When Congress rolled back much of the Inflation Reduction Act, local-factory credits survived and diffuse consumer credits didn't. Sign multi-year co-production deals with Japan and Australia so allies become stakeholders in continuity. Run delivery through a small White House unit with a monthly stocktake, and share its dashboard with both parties' committee leaders.

Count **deliveries, not "capacity."** Capacity is the easiest number to game.

### What I'd tell the President that he won't want to hear

**Drop the foreign-warship fight.** Both chambers' NDAA texts restrict buying battle-force ships from foreign yards ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). Fighting it spends capital on a losing battle and unites the shipbuilding-state coalition you need against you. Don't veto the NDAA over it. Take the allied *investment* instead.

**Slow the battleship.** CBO prices the first one at about $23bn and fifteen at $275bn. It also finds the plan more than doubles the large-surface-combatant tonnage yards must produce by 2035 ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62550)). That competes directly with the submarine and destroyer throughput you need sooner. Keep the brand and the design work, and defer the buy until submarine output recovers. This is the President's signature programme, which is exactly why someone has to say it.

**Admit the gap.** The deepest risk is a stopgap budget running into 2027 with no anomalies. It would quietly freeze every framework deal while the debt-limit fight, which CSIS expects in early-to-mid 2027, eats the calendar. If you only win one thing in the next three weeks, win the anomalies.

### The question for you

Each of these concessions costs the President something he wants: the $1.5T topline, the battleship, the foreign-yard fight, limits on pocket rescissions. Which would you give up first to make the arsenal core bipartisan, and why that one? Your answer tells you what you think the binding constraint really is.

---

**Statecraft lesson.** Capacity follows credible demand, not money. The Liberty ship miracle of the Second World War is remembered as wartime improvisation. In fact it rested on the 1936 Merchant Marine Act's decade of steady orders, and on British and French orders that expanded American plants before Pearl Harbor. When the moment came, the yards, suppliers and workers already existed. The pattern holds wherever lead times are long, from arsenals and power grids to chip fabs. The state's most valuable asset is a commitment that survives budget cycles, elections and its own authors. Executive will alone is a bet on one person staying in power. Law, contracts and constituencies are a bet on the system.
