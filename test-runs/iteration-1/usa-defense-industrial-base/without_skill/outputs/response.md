Here's the bottom line first. Money is no longer the main constraint. What's missing is demand that industry believes will last, the skilled workers to build things, and a few lower-tier suppliers that everything depends on. So the strategy I'd bring to the White House is to **trade topline for durability.** Stop trying to fund the industrial base through party-line, one-time money. Lock it into multi-year contracts that both parties co-own and that a future Congress would have to *pay* to unwind. Then cut the projects that compete for the same scarce shipyards and rocket-motor lines.

Since I can't ask you, here are my assumptions. You want a nonpartisan, actionable plan for the current administration. "Long war with China" means a long conventional fight in the Western Pacific. "Fix it" means meaningfully better capacity within this decade, not a World War II–style mobilization.

## 1. You're right about the problem. The Iran war proved it.

The U.S. and Israel began operations against Iran on February 28, 2026, and the first 38 days were major combat ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-05_IN12668_fdf32c75fda33d9d6a1e94bac5a2c3628b21898b.html)). An August report citing U.S. officials put interceptor use at about 1,700 Patriot, 200 THAAD and 150 Standard Missile interceptors. CBO estimates the U.S. has "probably used between one-half and two-thirds" of its inventory of certain interceptors. It says replacing them would probably take at least five years, even at higher production rates ([Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/09/15/iran-war-has-cost-38-billion-depleted-two-thirds-of-us-missile-interceptors-cbo-says/)). Offensive missiles took a similar hit. CSIS-based estimates put the war's use at roughly 1,000 Tomahawks against a prewar stock of about 3,100, and more than 1,000 JASSMs against about 4,400 ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/munitions-used-against-iran-take-years-replace-huge-pentagon-weapons-budget/)).

Now compare that with output. Lockheed delivered 620 PAC-3 missiles in **all of 2025**. Its new deal aims for 2,000 a year, and the CEO says that rate will be reached "by the end of 2030" ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-lockheed-martin-announce-plans-to-triple-pac-3-production/)). Six weeks against a mid-sized regional power used up multiple years of production. Against China, with its far larger missile force, the math gets worse fast. This was also a one-war-at-a-time problem. Estonia said U.S. deliveries were paused until the Iran fighting ended. U.S. Forces Korea said its munitions were being moved to the Middle East ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/munitions-used-against-iran-take-years-replace-huge-pentagon-weapons-budget/)).

Ships are worse. CBO says Virginia-class submarine production has averaged only 1.1 boats a year for the past two years, while the Navy has been buying about 2.0. Destroyers and submarines now take 9–10 years to build, against 5–6 in the 2000s. Delays "increased slightly from 2025 to 2026, despite substantial investments to reduce them" ([CBO, April 2026](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2026-04/62258-shipbuilding-challenges.pdf)). Meanwhile the PLA Navy has more than 370 ships and submarines. China's shipbuilding capacity is commonly estimated at roughly 230 times America's ([19FortyFive](https://www.19fortyfive.com/2026/04/china-has-230-times-the-shipbuilding-capacity-of-the-united-states-and-the-u-s-navy-is-shrinking-to-287-ships-while-china-passes-370/); [TWZ on the Navy intelligence slide](https://www.twz.com/alarming-navy-intel-slide-warns-of-chinas-200-times-greater-shipbuilding-capacity)).

So the diagnosis holds. Two parts of your framing would still lead to the wrong strategy.

## 2. Where your framing is off

**Correction one: this is no longer mainly a money problem.** The 2025 reconciliation law already added about $29.2 billion for shipbuilding and $25.4 billion for munitions and supply chains ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12580.html)). Shipbuilding delays still got worse. The clearest example is 155mm artillery shells. The Army spent $469 million on a new plant in Mesquite, Texas, that was meant to make 30,000 shell bodies a month. As of March 2026 it had produced **zero** parts that met contract specifications. Overall output was about 36,000 rounds a month against a goal of 100,000 that was supposed to be met by October 2025 ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/)). Money buys equipment. It doesn't buy a working production process, a trained workforce, or a supplier that can actually deliver. When a strategy reduces to "spend more," it fails for exactly this reason.

**Correction two: ships and munitions run on different clocks, and you need a separate plan for each.** Munitions are recoverable within this decade. The production lines exist, the bottlenecks are known (rocket motors, seekers, energetics, castings), and the PAC-3 deal shows a workable contract model. Shipbuilding is not recoverable on any timeline that matters for deterring China this decade. Even the White House's own maritime team says its plan will take "years, if not decades" to bear fruit ([USNI News](https://news.usni.org/2026/02/13/trump-administration-details-make-shipbuilding-great-again-effort-in-new-action-plan)). So the naval strategy can't be "out-build China." It has to be "get more out of the fleet we have, stop disrupting the yards, and use allied shipyards for what we can't do." If you blend the two problems into one "industrial base" story, you'll pour political capital into the slow one and starve the fast one.

## 3. Why the base behaves this way

Think of the defense industry as a supplier with one customer who constantly changes the order. Since the end of the Cold War, the Pentagon has signaled demand in annual, fragile amounts. Continuing resolutions forbid new programs and rate increases. New ship classes get announced and cancelled. Wartime demand spikes and then disappears. Faced with that, a defense company's CFO behaves rationally. They don't build a production line the government may stop funding in two years. They return cash to shareholders instead. The administration's January 7, 2026 executive order, which targets buybacks and dividends at underperforming contractors ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/01/capacity-over-dividends)), goes after the *symptom*. The *cause* is that the government's promises to keep buying haven't been credible.

Two structural problems sit beneath that. The first is single points of failure in the lower tiers. For submarines, about 70% of critical component suppliers have no competitor ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2026-04/62258-shipbuilding-challenges.pdf)). The Pentagon's $1 billion stake in L3Harris's solid-rocket-motor business ([GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/defense/2026/06/pentagons-growing-role-investor-scrutiny-capitol-hill/414304/)) exists because one lower-tier component can cap production of an entire missile family. The second is a labor market that has turned against the shipyards. Shipyards are roughly 174,000 workers short. First-year attrition runs 50–60%. According to a senior Navy acquisition official, shipyard pay has fallen from 3–4 times the pay of comparable work to 1.2–1.4 times ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2026/10/cogs-of-war/the-navys-missing-welders-are-stocking-shelves/)). If a welder earns only modestly more than a warehouse worker for much harder work, no shipbuilding budget fixes that.

**The strategic insight that follows:** the real asset you're building is *credible commitment*. Judge every policy by one question: does this make the government's promise to keep buying more believable to a CFO deciding whether to build a production line? That question drives both the policy and the politics below.

## 4. The policy: five moves

**Move one: turn munitions into a long-term production program set in law.** The FY2026 NDAA already made munitions eligible for multi-year procurement ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/)). In the FY2027 NDAA conference, the House bill authorizes multiyear buys for 13 munitions and the Senate bill for 19 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). Take the Senate's 19. Then copy the PAC-3 contract model across the list, because it works. It is a seven-year framework. It adjusts for inflation. It reimburses industry's one-time setup costs if the government or Congress cuts the deal short. It shares savings from stable demand. And it gets Lockheed to fund the expansion itself ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/01/pentagon-lockheed-martin-announce-plans-to-triple-pac-3-production/)). Push the same model down to component suppliers. The August deals did this: a second source for Patriot rocket motors and long-term frameworks with component makers, toward a goal of nearly 14,000 Patriot and THAAD interceptors ([Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2026/08/03/pentagon-moves-to-triple-patriot-quadruple-thaad-interceptor-production/)). The protection against cancellation is the key piece. It is what persuades a company to risk its own capital.

Add a bridge for the gap years. Adm. Paparo said scaling will take "one to two years" and "won't be soon enough." He called for nontraditional vendors and low-cost munitions ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/munitions-used-against-iran-take-years-replace-huge-pentagon-weapons-budget/)). For the next several years, deterrence depends on cheap, plentiful weapons, allied stockpiles, and hard choices about U.S. stockpiles versus foreign military sales. Ramping production alone won't cover it.

**Move two: for ships, get more out of the existing fleet before building new classes.** The cheapest new ships are the ones stuck in maintenance. CBO says 33% of attack submarines are in or awaiting maintenance. Hitting the 20% goal would raise deployable boats from 32 to 43, and meeting the old destroyer maintenance goal would add 12 deployable ships ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2026-04/62258-shipbuilding-challenges.pdf)). That's more combat power, sooner, than any construction program can deliver. Pair it with design stability and multi-year block buys for the classes already in production. Protect Columbia above everything else. The first boat is already 12–16 months late.

Here's the hard advice. **Don't start construction of the Trump-class battleship now.** CBO puts the first ship at more than $23 billion and the planned fleet at $275 billion. It says the first hull could take eight years or longer and that U.S. yards may lack the capacity to build it ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/06/g-s1-137662/trump-battleships-plan-cost); [gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/cbo-trump-class-nuclear-battleships-could-cost-275-billion-strain-u-s-shipbuilding/)). CBO has also warned that adding new classes to the pipeline "could further tax" production. A nuclear-powered surface combatant would compete directly with Columbia and Virginia for the same scarce nuclear-qualified workers and single-source suppliers. The politically survivable way to handle this is not cancellation. Recast it as a design-maturation program: fund the design, and tie construction to Columbia getting back on schedule. The President keeps the program and the shipyards keep their focus.

**Move three: put the workforce at the center.** The Navy is looking to hire 250,000 shipyard workers over ten years ([Naval News](https://www.navalnews.com/naval-news/2026/01/us-navy-seeks-to-hire-250000-new-dockyard-workers-in-the-next-10-years/)). Most current spending reshuffles experienced welders between employers instead of creating new ones. Fund a wage bridge that pays adults during short training programs. War on the Rocks estimates about $11,000 per worker, comparable to the roughly $10,000 per hire the submarine ad campaign spent. Tie it to completion and job-placement standards. Require Navy-funded programs to report placed workers' prior earnings, so you can tell whether a program creates workers or just poaches them. Give each defense manufacturing region one intermediary that is held accountable for results ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2026/10/cogs-of-war/the-navys-missing-welders-are-stocking-shelves/)). This is also the most valuable political asset in the plan, as section 5 explains.

**Move four: secure the inputs, with guardrails.** China suspended its rare-earth restrictions for one year in late October 2025. That suspension is now coming due, and U.S. imports never recovered to 2024 levels. The Pentagon's ban on Chinese rare earths and magnets starts January 1, 2027, and CSIS warns it may not be feasible without much more capacity ([CSIS](https://csis.org/analysis/rare-earth-export-restrictions-one-year-later)). The MP Materials model of an equity stake, a price floor and a guaranteed purchase is sound. But the Pentagon's equity stakes are about to face heavy oversight. The Senate bill would require congressional notification, cap direct stakes at 40% and $500 million per deal, and bar board seats ([GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/defense/2026/06/pentagons-growing-role-investor-scrutiny-capitol-hill/414304/)). **Accept those guardrails voluntarily.** An equity tool seen as cronyism gets defunded. One with clear rules survives.

**Move five: be disciplined about what you demand.** Every new commitment draws on the same limited capacity. CBO estimates Golden Dome could cost $1.2 trillion over 20 years ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/defense-news/2026/05/cbo-estimates-golden-dome-could-cost-1-2-trillion-over-20-years/)). The Pentagon disputes that figure, but even at lower cost it competes for the same rocket motors, seekers and engineers as the interceptors you just used up. Phase it. In the near term, prioritize interceptors that serve both homeland and theater defense, like PAC-3, THAAD and SM-6, ahead of space-based layers. The goal isn't to kill programs. It's to stop the industrial base from receiving several "top priorities" at once.

## 5. The politics and the budget

**Where things stand.** As of Nov 25, 10:34am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats have won 259 House seats and Republicans 173. 3 seats are counted as won for no party in the AP results I have, which don't say why or which way those seats will go. The label "Majority reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. In the Senate, as of Nov 25, 10:33am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats at a total of 56 and Republicans at a total of 44. The label "Control reached" beside the Democrats is not a call by AP. Those numbers may have changed since.

Going by AP's count of those seats, I'm planning on the basis that any lasting industrial-base program must be co-owned by Democrats on the defense committees from January 3, 2027 onward. Treat that as the core design constraint for everything below.

**The budget structure is the biggest strategic mistake on the table.** The FY2027 request totals $1.5 trillion. That is $1.1 trillion in regular discretionary spending, which needs some Senate Democratic votes, plus $350 billion in mandatory spending that would move through party-line reconciliation. Much of the industrial-base money sits in the reconciliation bucket: $113 billion for the industrial base, $47 billion for munitions, $53.6 billion for drones, and Golden Dome ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). The request was also drafted before the Iran war ([Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/munitions-used-against-iran-take-years-replace-huge-pentagon-weapons-budget/)). Holland & Knight judges a party-line reconciliation bill "increasingly less likely in the lame duck." Rosa DeLauro, the top Democrat on House Appropriations, has pushed to claw back money Republicans provided through reconciliation.

Apply the credible-commitment test. Money that can be clawed back is money no CFO will build a factory on. Even if the party-line route succeeds, it produces exactly the stop-start demand that caused this mess. **So the trade I'd recommend:** give ground on the topline fight, which Holland & Knight calls the single most contentious issue. In exchange, move munitions and industrial-base funding into the regular discretionary budget, with multi-year authority and bipartisan sponsorship. A somewhat smaller budget that both parties own beats a bigger one that one party can undo.

**Pull munitions replenishment away from the Iran fight.** The Iran supplemental, $67 billion for defense within an $87.6 billion request, is politically contested. Many Democrats want provisions to end hostilities ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/06/white-house-sends-87-6b-supplemental-to-congress-with-67b-for-defense/); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). If interceptor replenishment rides on that vehicle, it inherits the fight. Reframe replenishment as what it really is: restoring deterrence against China in the Indo-Pacific. Move it as its own measure. Members who oppose the Iran operation can still vote to rebuild the stockpile, and many will want to.

**Design the coalition so each side gets something it actually wants.** For Democrats, the offer is the workforce title, ideally written by their own members, along with real oversight: a public production scoreboard, equity-stake guardrails, and accountability for contractors who take public money and miss delivery dates. The buyback issue is a rare overlap between left and right. The Senate NDAA already bars defense-company buybacks and dividends. Offer to codify a narrower, performance-linked version of the executive order rather than fight it. For Republicans, the offer is deterrence, acquisition reform, and manufacturing jobs. Then let geography carry it. Munitions plants and shipyards are spread across both parties' states: Connecticut and Virginia yards, Pennsylvania shell plants in Scranton and Wilkes-Barre, Hanwha's Philadelphia yard, missile lines across the South and Southwest. Every senator with a production line has a reason to make the program permanent.

**Pick fights carefully.** OMB wants to order two warships and two support ships from foreign yards. Both chambers' NDAA bills restrict foreign-built Navy vessels ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/index.php/article/white-house-pushes-congress-to-build-warships-and-non-combatants-overseas); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook)). Foreign-built warships are a fight you will lose and that burns goodwill. Narrow the ask to auxiliaries and support ships under the "Finland Model": the first hulls are built abroad and the rest at U.S. yards, conditioned on allied investment in American shipyards. Korea's $150 billion shipbuilding pledge makes that credible. It turns an argument about "jobs going overseas" into a story about foreign capital coming in.

**Lame-duck sequencing.** The current funding resolution expires December 11, and Holland & Knight says the risk of a year-long CR for the Pentagon has risen. A year-long CR would hit munitions hardest, because CRs block new programs and production-rate increases, which is the entire plan. The first priority is therefore either a full-year defense appropriation or, at minimum, specific CR exceptions for multiyear munitions contracts and rate increases. The second is to finish the NDAA in conference with the Senate's munitions list intact, a buyback compromise tied to performance, and the foreign-yard compromise. The third is to put the remaining 2025 reconciliation money for munitions and shipbuilding on contract quickly, through multi-year frameworks with cancellation protection. It must still meet the spending deadline of September 30, 2029 ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12580.html)). Money already on contract for real production capacity is far harder to claw back than unspent balances. Pair it with full transparency, so it reads as execution rather than evasion.

## 6. Making it stick

Policy tools differ hugely in how long they last. Executive orders get reversed by the next administration. Appropriations last a year. Authorizations last until someone amends them. **Signed multi-year contracts with cancellation protection** are the most durable tool available, because unwinding them costs money and a future Congress has to vote to pay it. So convert as much of the program as possible from executive orders and annual money into statute and long-term contracts.

Next, give it an institutional home that outlasts the current Pentagon leadership. Put the production mission, now spread across bodies like the Munitions Acceleration Council and the Economic Defense Unit, under a statutory office with a Senate-confirmed head. Then mandate a public scoreboard reported to Congress every quarter. It should track monthly output for each critical munition against target, ship delivery delays, maintenance availability, and first-year shipyard attrition. Scoreboards build durability because they create constituencies that notice when numbers slip. They also give the other party a reason to defend the program instead of attacking it: oversight becomes their stake in its success.

Finally, lock in allies. Coproduction agreements and allied investment commitments carry diplomatic costs if reversed, which makes them stickier than domestic budget lines. They also add capacity faster than new American factories can.

## 7. How this fails, and what most people get wrong

It fails if demand keeps whipsawing: a year-long CR, a new ship class every budget cycle, several simultaneous "top priorities." It fails if the tools get politicized. An equity stake that looks like cronyism, or a flagship ship that looks like vanity, hands the opposition a reason to defund the whole effort. It fails if money is confused with capacity, as Mesquite showed. And it fails if an outside shock hits before the redundancy exists, such as the rare-earth suspension lapsing or a second conflict before stockpiles recover.

The most common mistake is focusing on the big prime contractors. The real constraints sit in the lower tiers: rocket motors, seekers, forgings, and the welder who left for a warehouse job. The second mistake is punishing shareholder returns without fixing the cause. A blanket buyback ban raises defense firms' cost of capital and pushes investors away. Performance-linked restrictions combined with cancellation protection change behavior without starving the sector. The third mistake is treating this as a ship-count race with China. That race can't be won this decade. The near-term contest is about munitions depth, fleet availability, and allied capacity.

**The big-picture implication.** For the rest of this decade, deterrence against China will rest less on how many hulls the U.S. can launch and more on whether it can sustain a high-intensity missile war past the first few weeks. Iran showed that it currently can't. The fix exists, and much of it is already in motion: the PAC-3 model, the munitions multiyear authority, the equity tools. What's missing is the political structure that makes industry believe it will last. The White House's leverage is to give up the fight for the biggest budget and win the fight for the most durable one.
