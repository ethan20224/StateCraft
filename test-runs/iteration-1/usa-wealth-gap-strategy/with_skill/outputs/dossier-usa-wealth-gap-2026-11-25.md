# Strategy Dossier: Closing America's Wealth Gap
## The Ownership Compact, 2026–2036

**Prepared for:** The President
**Date:** 25 November 2026 · Version 1
**Status:** For decision. Figures are as of the dates cited. Every probability here is a judgment call, not a measurement.

## 0. Cabinet Brief

**Bottom line.** The wealth gap as it is usually measured, the top 1%'s share of national wealth, cannot be "fixed" in one presidency by any policy that can pass, because that number moves mainly with the stock market. What can be done, starting now, is to change who owns America. Every child can be made an owner, automatically and progressively, on accounts this administration has already built. The bottom half's largest asset can be protected from the 2032 Social Security cliff. And it can be paid for by making the very top play by the same rules as everyone else.

**The recommended strategy.** Turn Trump Accounts from an opt-in, flat, temporary program into an automatic, permanent and progressive account that lasts a lifetime. Every newborn is seeded at birth with no paperwork. Children in low-income families get a top-up every year. At 18 the account rolls into the young adult's own retirement account, where the new Saver's Match keeps adding to it. Pay for it with a 39.6% rate on income above $1 million and with carried-interest reform, both positions you have floated before, plus restored IRS enforcement at the high end. In the same Congress, sign the bipartisan PROMISE Act, so that Congress is forced to vote on Social Security solvency before the 2032 cut. Write a hard firewall into it: the accounts add to Social Security and never carve money out of it. Every piece is designed so both parties can claim it, because in America the only policies that survive the next election are the ones both parties can claim.

**Decisions required from you.**
1. **The mission.** Is it the floor (everyone owns something real) or the ceiling (no one owns too much of the country)? This dossier assumes the floor, and handles the ceiling through "same rules" financing. That is a choice of values, not a technical question, and it is yours to make.
2. **The formula.** Will you accept a progressive formula inside your signature program in exchange for making it permanent and automatic? Recommended: yes.
3. **The pay-for threshold.** A 39.6% rate above $1 million pays for the whole package. Above $2.5 million it raises about a tenth as much. Recommended: open at $1 million.
4. **Social Security.** Will you endorse the PROMISE Act fast-track and a written firewall against carve-outs? Recommended: yes.
5. **The name.** Will you let the low-income top-up carry a bipartisan name while the account keeps yours? Recommended: yes.

**The single biggest risk.** The package turns into a 2028 campaign football. Democrats refuse to give you a signing ceremony, anti-tax Republicans block the pay-for, and Trump Accounts stay opt-in and flat. They then drift into the pattern of 529 college-savings plans, where the families who need help least get the most. That drift is already visible: by mid-June only about 39% of eligible children had claimed the $1,000 seed ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/trump-accounts-surpass-6-million-but-signs-of-a-wealth-gap-stoke-concerns/267118)).

**Odds line for Pillar 1, the Ownership Act.** Works 0.6 · Passes 0.4 · Delivered 0.7 · Lasts 0.7, which multiplies out to about 12%. Binding gate: Passes. For comparison, a conventional stand-alone baby-bonds bill in this Congress scores about 0.6%, and a federal wealth tax about 0.1%. Across all three pillars, the chance of at least one durable, meaningful win by 2032 is roughly one in three.

**This week.** Make decisions 1 to 5. Name a Treasury Deputy Secretary to run an Ownership Delivery Unit. Have the Treasury Secretary and the Chief of Staff open quiet talks with three groups: the authors of the auto-enrolment, expansion and baby-bonds bills; the leaders of both parties for the new Congress; and the bipartisan PROMISE Act sponsors. Announce nothing until a bipartisan pair of lead sponsors is locked in.

## 1. The Problem, Reframed

### What is actually happening

The headline numbers are stark. The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts (DFA) for the second quarter of 2026 show the top 1% holding 32.5% of household wealth, the highest share in records going back to 1989. The top 0.1% alone held 15%. The bottom half held 2.3%, down from 3.5% in 1989 and 2.7% in 2022 ([24/7 Wall St., citing the Fed DFA](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/)). Total household and nonprofit net worth stood at $195.9 trillion ([Fed Z.1](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/20260911/recent_developments.htm)). That puts everything the bottom half of American households owns, combined, at roughly $4–4.5 trillion.

The 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), released on 9 October, fills in the detail ([Federal Reserve](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20261009a.htm)):
- **The middle barely moved while the top surged.** Median family net worth rose only 2% from 2022, to $215,900, while the mean rose 7%, to $1.24 million. The median net worth of the richest tenth of families rose 31%, to $3.6 million ([ITV](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-09/stock-market-surge-lifts-75-plus-families-to-top-of-us-wealth-while-young-households-slip)).
- **Fewer families own stocks, and more are under debt stress.** Stock-market participation fell from 58% to 56%. The share of families spending more than 40% of income on debt payments rose to 8.6%, the highest since 2013.
- **Black families went backwards.** Black household net worth fell 25% over the same three years ([theGrio](https://thegrio.com/2026/10/14/black-wealth-drops-25-trumps-agenda-is-making-the-squeeze-worse/)).
- **The young are being squeezed out of homeownership.** First-time buyers made up just 22% of purchases this year, near the record low, and their median age hit a record 40 ([NAR via Inman](https://www.inman.com/2026/11/05/first-time-homebuyer-data-nar/)).
- **The long arc is decline.** About 90% of Americans born in 1940 out-earned their parents, against about 50% of those born in the 1980s. Faster growth alone would not restore the old rate; growth shared as broadly as it was for the 1940 cohort would reverse more than 70% of the decline ([Chetty et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22910)).

### "The wealth gap" is four different problems

Each of these has its own levers, its own coalition and its own enemies.

**1. Concentration at the top.** This is the share of the country owned by the richest 1% and 0.1%. The levers are tax, competition (antitrust) policy and the rules on money in politics. The opponents are the best-organised interests in the country.

**2. The bottom half owns almost nothing.** This is less a gap than an absence: little home equity, few financial assets, and rising debt stress. The levers are defaults, starting capital, homeownership and protection from financial shocks.

**3. The racial wealth gap.** It is rooted in decades of exclusion from America's main asset-building programs, and it widened again when the 2022–25 rise in stock prices went mostly to families that already owned stocks.

**4. Stalled mobility and the generational squeeze.** The young own a shrinking share, buy their first home about a decade later than earlier generations did, and are less likely to out-earn their parents.

**This dossier attacks the second problem, with the third and fourth carried along by the same design.** There are four reasons.
- **It can actually be moved.** Policy can put real assets into the hands of the bottom half within a decade. No policy that can pass will materially shrink the top 1%'s share in that time.
- **It is where values meet.** Pew found in August that 72% of Americans, including 54% of Republicans, want the federal government to take "a lot of responsibility" for reducing inequality ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2026/10/14/americans-views-of-economic-inequality-its-causes-and-who-should-fix-it/)). "Ownership" is a word the right uses as easily as the left.
- **It can be measured** with data the government already collects.
- **It needs no race-based eligibility.** A design targeted on family wealth and income narrows the racial and generational gaps without race-conscious rules, which would face serious legal risk after *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023).

### The trap in the headline number

The top 1% hold 50.9% of the stocks and mutual funds that households own outside retirement plans, while the bottom half's wealth is mostly home equity and cash. So the top 1%'s share is, roughly speaking, a thermometer of stock prices measured against house prices. In the second quarter of 2026 alone, household stock holdings rose by $10.7 trillion. That is more than double everything the bottom half owns.

Suppose the government sets a target for the top 1%'s share. A bear market would hit that target faster than any policy could, and a bull market would blow straight through it whatever the policy did. That is a target begging to be gamed or made meaningless, which is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. So the dashboard in section 12 tracks the top share but sets its targets at the bottom: ownership, median wealth, the wealth of young adults by race and income, and homeownership.

### The asset nobody counts

The Fed's wealth figures leave out Social Security, which is the bottom half's single largest store of wealth. Counting it changes the picture. Sylvain Catherine of Wharton and his co-authors found that the top 1%'s share rose 7.6 percentage points from 1989 to 2019 in the Fed's figures, but only 1.5 points once Social Security wealth is included. Over the same period Social Security wealth grew from $7 trillion to more than $40 trillion ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/social-security-reduced-wealth-inequality-could-be-insolvent-2032/)).

Two conclusions follow, and neither camp will like both of them.
- **The right has a point:** the official figures overstate how much inequality in total economic security has grown.
- **The left has a point too:** this makes Social Security's solvency a wealth-gap question of the first order. The 2026 Trustees project the retirement trust fund will run out in the fourth quarter of 2032. After that, incoming revenue covers about 78% of scheduled benefits, which means a 22% cut ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/06/09/social-security-trust-fund-medicare)).

**The biggest scheduled widening of the wealth gap in the next decade is not a tax cut or a market boom. It is that cliff.**

### Root causes

The root causes fall into three groups.

- **The bottom half holds few assets that grow in value.**
  - Low, volatile income leaves little to save, and debt stress is now the highest since 2013.
  - Nearly half of private-sector workers have no workplace retirement plan, so there is no default saving ([401k Specialist](https://401kspecialistmag.com/state-auto-ira-programs-eclipse-3-billion-in-retirement-assets/)).
  - Too few homes get built. The result is a first home at 40, if at all.
  - Shocks such as medical bills and job loss force families to sell what little they own. The 2025 reconciliation law cut Medicaid and SNAP resources by a net $900 billion. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimates the bottom tenth of households lose about $1,200 a year and the top tenth gain about $13,600 ([Thomson Reuters on CBO](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/cbo-analyzes-distributional-effects-of-budget-act/)).
  - There is no starting capital. Wealth passes down through families, which is the main channel of the racial gap.
- **Wealth at the top compounds faster.**
  - The top owns the stocks, and returns flow to whoever owns the capital.
  - The tax code favours gains that are never sold. Under "step-up in basis," gains are wiped clean at death; the Joint Committee on Taxation puts the cost at about $72.5 billion in 2026 ([PGPF](https://www.pgpf.org/blog/2021/06/what-is-the-stepped-up-basis-and-why-does-the-biden-administration-want-to-eliminate-it)). This is the "buy, borrow, die" strategy: borrow against appreciated assets instead of selling them, then pass them on untaxed. Fund managers' carried interest, meanwhile, is taxed at capital-gains rates.
  - Enforcement has eroded. More than 25,000 IRS staff left in 2025, enforcement and collection staffing fell 27%, and partnership audits fell 76% between 2023 and 2025 ([OPB/NPR on the TIGTA report](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/03/more-taxes-have-gone-uncollected-after-irs-slashed-its-staff/)).
- **Feedback loops keep the pattern in place.**
  - Saving incentives delivered through the tax code flow to people who already save. Fewer than 1% of lower-income families with young children own a 529 college-savings account, against nearly three in ten of the highest-income families ([Hamilton in Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2026/07/16/trump-accounts-rich-families-529-data/)).
  - Wealth buys political voice, and voice protects the tax rules that favour wealth.

### Who benefits from the status quo

- Owners of large unrealised gains and the industry that advises them.
- Private-equity and hedge-fund managers on carried interest.
- Existing homeowners and the local zoning coalitions that protect property values.
- Less obviously, politicians in both parties who find the issue more useful than a solution.

Expect the first two groups to fight the financing, and the third to fight housing reform. The fourth group will fight anything that hands the other side a win.

### The cost of doing nothing

The status quo is not stable; it is heading somewhere worse.
- **The Social Security cliff.** In 2032 an automatic 22% cut hits retirement benefits, the main asset of the bottom half.
- **The 2025 reconciliation law.** Its effects keep moving resources from the bottom tenth to the top tenth every year.
- **Trump Accounts as currently designed.** Take-up of the $1,000 seed is about 39%, and the program is open to contributions of up to $5,000 a year, so it will widen gaps in children's balances rather than close them. A typical account seeded with $1,000 grows to about $4,000 by adulthood. A child whose parents put in the maximum each year ends up with many times that.

## 2. The Seat and the Constraints

**The principal.** The President of the United States, with 26 months left in a final term.

**What the seat can actually do.**
- **The veto,** which in divided government is the main bargaining chip.
- **Rulemaking through Treasury.** Temporary rules issued on 30 September already let Treasury open Trump Accounts automatically for about 73 million children ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/trump-accounts-treasury-automatic-child-enrollment.amp)). Treasury is also implementing the Saver's Match, and the TrumpIRA.gov site launches on 1 January 2027 ([401k Specialist](https://401kspecialistmag.com/treasury-irs-begin-rulemaking-for-2027-savers-match-advance-trumpira-gov/)).
- **The budget proposal and the bully pulpit.**
- **Unusual control of delivery.** One official, Frank Bisignano, serves as both IRS chief executive and Social Security Commissioner, and has been put in charge of the next phase of Trump Accounts ([Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0563)). For a program that needs birth records from SSA and income data from the IRS, that is a real advantage.
- **A small portfolio of federal equity stakes,** including about 10% of Intel (worth nearly $11 billion) and 15% of MP Materials ([Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/01/14/trump-state-capitalism-equity-stakes-list-companies/)).

**What it cannot do.** The President cannot create permanent mandatory deposits or change tax rates without a statute. Regulations can be undone by the next administration, and since *Loper Bright* (2024) ended judicial deference to agencies, they are easier to challenge in court. The auto-enrolment rules in particular should be written into law.

**The Congress.** As of Nov 25, 10:35am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with 259 House seats won and Republicans with 173 won. Three House seats are counted as won for neither party in the AP results I have, and those results don't say why or which way those seats will go. In the Senate, AP's count as of the same time shows Democrats with a total of 56 (which includes two others who caucus with them) and Republicans with a total of 44. These numbers may have changed since then. The label "Majority reached" beside the Democrats in the House is not a call by AP, and neither is the label "Control reached" beside them in the Senate.

Going by AP's count, this dossier plans for divided government from January. That is a planning assumption, not the only possible case.

Two structural facts matter more than any margin. First, every bill needs your signature, or a two-thirds override in both chambers. Second, most legislation still needs 60 votes to end a Senate filibuster: as of August, the filibuster had survived the President's 2026 push to abolish it ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/08/03/the-not-so-secret-filibuster-society/)). The result is that budget reconciliation, the party-line route behind the 2017, 2022 and 2025 laws, is not available without you. Anything that passes in 2027–28 passes as a bipartisan deal.

**Political capital.** Low. Post-midterm polls put approval at 32% (Economist/YouGov, 9–11 November, and Reuters/Ipsos, 6–9 November), and the New York Times average is 36% ([Newsweek](https://newsweek.com/donald-trump-approval-rating-falling-midterm-losses-12709719)). Low capital argues for moves where the other side's interests already line up with yours, not confrontations. But history offers a pattern. Presidents facing a hostile Congress late in their tenure often turn to legacy deals: Reagan's 1986 tax reform passed with a Democratic House, and Clinton's 1996–97 welfare reform and children's health program passed with a Republican Congress.

**The clock.**
- **The usable legislative window** runs roughly from January 2027 to the summer of 2028, after which the presidential campaign takes over the floor.
- **Must-pass bills** (annual appropriations, and any debt-limit deal) are fallback vehicles to attach this to.
- **The Social Security deadline** in late 2032 falls in the next presidency, but the machinery for meeting it can be built in this one.

**Fiscal room.** Tight. Debt held by the public was 99% of GDP at the end of fiscal 2025 and is projected to reach a record 120% by 2036. Net interest costs go from $970 billion in 2025 to $2.1 trillion in 2036 ([CRFB on CBO](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cbo-releases-february-2026-budget-and-economic-outlook)). New mandatory spending should be fully paid for, for three reasons: the bond market (the lesson of Liz Truss), the CBO score, and the fairness story that keeps the program alive.

**Legal limits.**
- **Wealth tax.** *Moore v. United States* (2024) left open whether income must be "realised" before it can be taxed, so a federal wealth tax invites a constitutional fight.
- **Race-conscious eligibility.** It now invites one too.
- **Spending and income-tax changes** carry little legal risk.

**Capacity.** The IRS, which will run Saver's Match payments in 2028, has lost more than a quarter of its enforcement and collection staff. The administration has proposed further cuts for 2027.

**Outside conditions.**
- **Tariff revenue is unreliable.** The Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers (IEEPA) tariffs on 20 February 2026, and a fight over roughly $170 billion in refunds is under way ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/tariff-news/tariff-ruling-kicks-off-messy-fight-over-170-billion-in-refunds)).
- **Stock prices are high.** That is a timing risk for accounts invested only in stocks.

**If you meant a different seat.** A new President in January 2029 with their party controlling both chambers could use reconciliation for the low-income deposit and for step-up reform, and would own the 2032 Social Security deal. In that seat the binding gate shifts from Passes to Lasts, because the next 51-vote majority can unwind whatever the last one passed. The Speaker or Senate leader in the new Congress would use appropriations and the President's own program as leverage. I can rerun the plan from either chair.

## 3. What History Teaches

**Peaceful, deliberate compression of wealth is rare and slow.** The great levelling of the twentieth century came from depression and world war, followed by decades of steep progressive taxation. What policy can reliably do is spread ownership broadly. The postwar United States did exactly that through the GI Bill and federally backed 30-year mortgages, the largest asset-building effort in American history. It also built the racial wealth gap, because local administration and redlining kept Black families out. The lesson that transfers: build assets broadly, through rails that cannot be administered selectively.

**Defaults beat incentives.** The single most effective savings policy of recent decades was not a tax break but automatic enrolment in 401(k) plans. State auto-IRA programs, which apply the same idea to workers whose employers offer no plan, now hold more than $3 billion across more than 1.2 million funded accounts. Saving tax breaks that people have to opt into flow to people who already save, as the 529 data shows. The lesson: automatic, with no form to fill in.

**The UK's Child Trust Fund (2005–2011) is the closest precedent, and a warning.** Every British child received a government voucher at birth, with more for low-income families. The coalition government abolished it in its first austerity round, and many matured accounts later went unclaimed because families had never engaged with them. Two lessons follow. A program whose beneficiaries are diffuse, future and unaware is the first thing cut. And accounts opened automatically still need visible statements and a clear claim path at 18.

**Connecticut proved the design at state scale.** CT Baby Bonds automatically invests $3,200 for every baby whose birth is covered by the state's Medicaid program. The money can be used between ages 18 and 30 for a home, education, a business or retirement, and is projected to grow to $11,000–24,000. More than 33,000 children are enrolled, with $485 million under management ([CT Treasurer](https://portal.ct.gov/ott/newsroom/news/news-releases/ct-baby-bonds-turns-two-over-33000-children-now-eligible)). Two features transfer directly: targeting by Medicaid-covered birth, which is simple and needs no new means test, and a restricted list of uses.

**George W. Bush's 2005 push for personal accounts died of a single flaw.** They were to be carved out of Social Security, which turned every senior into an opponent. The lesson: the firewall is not optional.

**The 1983 Social Security rescue is the template for 2032.** A bipartisan commission met a hard insolvency deadline, and Reagan and House Speaker Tip O'Neill jointly owned the result. Neither could be blamed alone, and so the deal passed. The PROMISE Act recreates that structure.

**The Earned Income Tax Credit shows what lasts in America.** Reagan praised it as pro-work and pro-family; Democrats expanded it as anti-poverty policy. Because both parties could claim it, it has grown under presidents of both parties for half a century. Compare the expanded Child Tax Credit of 2021: delivered quickly through existing IRS payment systems, it cut child poverty to a record low, then lapsed because it was temporary and owned by one party.

**Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend** shows that a universal dividend builds a fierce constituency. It also shows that once budgets tighten, lawmakers will cut payouts whose size they control. The lesson: put the formula in statute and make the spending mandatory.

**Singapore's CPF and HDB system** made most citizens owners. It did so with compulsory savings on a scale no American Congress would legislate, and with state ownership of most land. Copying that model would be isomorphic mimicry: borrowing the form without the conditions. What does transfer is one account for life and the use of savings for a first home.

**Mexico's Progresa** survived a change of ruling party under a new name, partly because built-in independent evaluation made it hard to scrap. The lesson: mandate independent evaluation, and accept that a future government may rename the program.

**The 2026 tax fights at state level settle the wealth-tax question for now.** California's Proposition 40 was a one-time 5% tax on fortunes above $1 billion, touching about 200 people. News outlets including KTVU report that voters in the bluest large state rejected it, after an opposition campaign spending about $1 million a day and with the Democratic governor opposed ([KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/prop-40-californias-billionaire-tax-fails-ballot-box)). Massachusetts' 4% surtax on income above $1 million, by contrast, brought in more than $3.1 billion in ten months against early projections of about $2 billion a year, although the evidence on whether wealthy residents left is disputed ([CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/05/27/massachusetts-reels-in-3-1-billion-from-millionaires-tax/184020/)). The lesson: tax very high incomes, not the stock of wealth.

**Who wins and who pays.** The benefits are diffuse, spread across tens of millions of families with children and low-income savers. Normally that is weak politics. This design makes the benefits concentrated and visible: a balance with each child's name on it, an industry with assets under management to defend, and philanthropists and employers with money already in. The costs fall heavily on a small group with very high incomes and on private-equity managers. That is classic interest-group politics, where a small group with a lot to lose usually beats a large group with a little to gain. The counter is presidential cover plus a fairness story ("same rules"), and keeping the losers' bill small and predictable.

**Veto points.** The President (on side by design), the Senate filibuster, the House floor, the tax-writing committees (Finance and Ways and Means), CBO scoring, Treasury implementation, and the courts (low risk).

**The coalition path.** In the Senate: Democrats, plus the "ownership" Republicans, plus enough Republicans who follow the President's endorsement to reach cloture. In the House: Democratic leadership plus Republicans who follow the President. The deal has to let each side tell its base it won. The President's side gets permanence and expansion of his program. Democrats get the progressive formula and millionaires paying for it.

Cash for the bottom** (revive the expanded Child Tax Credit; "tariff dividend" checks) | 0.15 *for wealth*: cash raises income and consumption, not ownership | 0.25 | 0.85: existing IRS payment systems | 0.2: the 2021 credit lapsed; tariff revenue legally unstable | ~0.6% | Good anti-poverty policy, wrong tool for wealth. Hold in reserve for a recession. |
| **C. The Ownership Compact** (progressive, automatic, permanent accounts; Saver's Match delivered; Social Security floor; same-rules financing) | 0.6 | 0.4 | 0.7 | 0.7 | **~12%** | **Recommend.** |
| **D. Citizens' Wealth Fund** (pool federal equity stakes and pay the returns into every account, Alaska-style) | 0.3: far too small for decades | 0.15 | 0.6 | 0.6 | ~1.6% | A cheap add-on, not a strategy. Direct a share of returns from equity stakes into the accounts' common trust. |

**Why C wins.** It is the only option where three things overlap: the principal's own incentives, a delivery system that already exists, and a cross-party coalition. That overlap is the "change space" that Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock call authority, acceptance and ability.

**How each design choice moves the gates.** The baseline is a conventional Democratic baby-bonds bill introduced as a new program in this Congress: Works 0.6 · Passes 0.05 · Delivered 0.5 · Lasts 0.4, about 0.6%.
- **Building on Trump Accounts instead of creating a new program.** Passes rises from 0.05 to about 0.3, because the President owns the brand. Delivered rises from 0.5 to 0.7, because the rails exist and are already being auto-enrolled.
- **Financing from positions the President has floated.** Passes rises from about 0.3 to 0.4. Lasts rises from 0.4 to 0.6, thanks to fiscal credibility and the fairness story.
- **Statutory auto-enrolment, mandatory spending, the firewall and two-party naming.** Lasts rises from 0.6 to 0.7.

Together these take the odds from under 1% to about 12%. The progressive top-up is what keeps Works at 0.6. Without it (a "lite" bill that only codifies automatic, permanent seeding), the odds are similar (Works 0.3 · Passes 0.65 · Delivered 0.8 · Lasts 0.75, about 12%), but the impact is roughly half. The lite bill is the fallback, not the goal.

**The Economist and the Whip disagree about the pay-for.** The Economist wants the 39.6% bracket to start at $1 million: Penn Wharton scores it at $222.4 billion over ten years, enough to fund the whole of Pillar 1. The version the President reportedly floated in 2025, starting at $2.5 million for single filers ($5 million for married couples), raises just $22.3 billion ([Penn Wharton](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/p/2025-05-05-raising-top-ordinary-rates-options-for-reform-under-tcja-extension/)). The Whip answers that every dollar of tax increase costs Republican votes, and that only positions with the President's personal endorsement are safe.

The resolution is to open at $1 million and accept a settlement between $1 million and $2.5 million. Fill any shortfall with carried-interest reform and scored IRS enforcement revenue. Never fund the low-income top-up with borrowing: a deficit-financed version would be weaker on the fairness story, weaker on fiscal credibility and easier to repeal. If no pay-for survives, pass the lite bill and hold the top-up for 2029.

## 6. The Strategy

**Theory of change.** Suppose three things happen. Every child automatically gets an invested account, funded more generously for low-income families, which becomes their own retirement account as an adult. The bottom half's largest existing asset, Social Security, is protected from the 2032 cut. And the very top pays under the same rules as everyone else. Then the share of Americans who own appreciating assets will rise steadily, and the racial and generational gaps among young adults will narrow sharply. That follows from three facts: wealth compounds from early capital, defaults determine who saves, and a guaranteed public pension is the bottom half's main store of wealth. Paying for it through fair rules at the top keeps it fiscally credible and seen as fair, and that is what keeps it alive.

### Pillar 1: An account for every American (the Ownership Act of 2027)

1. **Automatic at birth, written into law.** When parents request a Social Security number at the hospital (the Enumeration at Birth process), SSA sends the record to Treasury, which opens the account. This is the Capito–Justice proposal ([NAPA](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/8/new-bill-would-pair-trump-account-creation-with-newborn-ssn-process/)). The $1,000 seed is deposited automatically, ending the separate election that has held take-up to about 39%. As things stand, auto-opened accounts get no seed without that election, and parents must claim them before any family or employer money can flow in.
2. **A permanent seed** beyond the 2028 end of the current pilot, indexed to inflation. Cost: about $3.6 billion a year at 2025's 3,606,400 births ([CDC](https://cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20260409.html)).
3. **An annual top-up for low-income children.** Up to $1,000 a year for children in families below the poverty line, phasing out at 300% of it, through age 17, indexed. Eligibility comes from data the government already holds: the prior year's tax return, or Medicaid or SNAP enrolment for families who don't file. It starts with newborn cohorts, so the program grows one age group at a time, which also makes it a natural built-in pilot. Cost: roughly $100–125 billion over the first decade. That is scaled from the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget's (CRFB) scoring of the larger Booker baby-bonds plan, which it put at about $250 billion over ten years for children born after enactment ([CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cory-bookers-baby-bonds-plan)). The real figure needs a CBO and Joint Committee on Taxation score. Give the top-up a name of its own, an "Opportunity Deposit", that Democrats can claim.
4. **A one-time catch-up deposit** for children under 10 in families below 200% of the poverty line. It complements the Dells' $250 pledge for children in lower- and middle-income ZIP codes.
5. **One account for life.** By law these accounts are already a new type of individual retirement account. At 18 the account becomes the young adult's own IRA. Between 18 and 30 it can be used for a first home, education or training, or starting a business, as in Connecticut. Otherwise it stays invested. From 2028, Saver's Match deposits flow into the same account.
6. **Protect children near 18 from a market crash.** The law currently requires U.S. stock index funds with fees capped at 0.10%. Add a default "glide path" that shifts gradually into safer assets from about age 13, so a child turning 18 in a crash year is not wiped out. This needs a change to the statute.
7. **Firewalls.**
   - No diversion of payroll taxes and no offset against Social Security benefits.
   - Balances are excluded from the asset tests for SNAP, Medicaid and Supplemental Security Income, so saving never costs a family its benefits.
   - No increase in the $5,000 annual contribution cap. Raising it would tilt the program further toward the affluent.
8. **Visibility.** Every family gets an annual statement showing the child's name and balance. This is the remedy for what political scientist Suzanne Mettler calls the "submerged state": benefits delivered through the tax code that people never see as government help and therefore never defend.
9. **Independent evaluation.** An annual public report by income and wealth band, prepared by an independent body with Census and Fed data access.

**What it adds up to.** A child from a poor family who receives the full top-up from birth reaches 18 with roughly $25,000–30,000 in today's dollars, assuming real returns of 3–5% a year. A child with only the flat seed reaches about $4,000. That difference is the strategy.

### Pillar 2: Protect the floor

1. **The PROMISE Act, enacted in 2027.** Introduced on 14 July by Sens. Durbin, Cassidy, Kaine, Tillis, King, Cornyn, Coons and Armstrong, it sets up a fast-track ([Durbin](https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/durbin-cassidy-kaine-tillis-king-cornyn-coons-armstrong-introduce-bipartisan-proposal-to-initiate-congressional-action-on-social-security)). The Social Security Advisory Board sends Congress a base bill that keeps the system solvent for at least 50 years. If committees don't act, it is automatically discharged. After 100 hours of debate it gets an up-or-down vote, with 60 votes needed in the Senate.

   Your red lines: no benefit cuts for the bottom half, and a large share of the fix from the top. SSA's actuaries score applying the payroll tax to earnings above $400,000, with benefit credit, as closing about 51% of the 75-year shortfall ([SSA OACT](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/solvency/provisions/payrolltax_summary.html)). Durbin and Tillis retire in January, so the coalition needs new champions now.
2. **Saver's Match, delivered well.** It pays a 50% match on the first $2,000 of retirement contributions, up to $1,000 a year, straight into the saver's account. Contributions count from 2027, and first payments arrive in 2028. Anyone eligible who has no account should be enrolled by default in a low-cost IRA listed on TrumpIRA.gov, so that no match is lost for lack of an account.
3. **A national automatic-IRA fallback** for workers whose employer offers no plan, building on the state programs, which hold more than $3 billion. Nearly half of private-sector workers still lack access.
4. **Do no further harm.** Hold the line on health-shock protection for the bottom half. Reversing the 2025 Medicaid and SNAP cuts is not achievable with this principal, and this dossier says so plainly (see section 14).

### Pillar 3: Same rules at the top

1. **In 2027,** as the pay-for for Pillar 1:
   - a 39.6% rate on income above $1 million;
   - carried interest taxed as ordinary income;
   - restored IRS capacity to audit high-wealth individuals and partnerships, whose audits fell 76%. Tax policy expert Natasha Sarin estimates the top 1% account for about a third of the "tax gap" between taxes owed and taxes paid.
2. **In 2029–32,** riding on the Social Security deal or the next major tax bill, close the "buy, borrow, die" route:
   - tax gains at death above a high exemption, with deferral for family farms and businesses that keep operating;
   - treat borrowing against very large appreciated holdings as a sale for tax purposes.
3. **Not a wealth tax.**

### Supporting plank: Homes

The federal lever is weak here. Zoning is local, and the President just let the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act become law without his signature, having tied signing it to an unrelated voter-ID bill ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/affordable-housing/road-to-housing-enacted-135272)). The practical steps:
- Implement the Act's $200 million-a-year competitive grant against measured increases in housing permits, not plans.
- Enforce the new caps on institutional investors buying single-family homes.
- Back a state-first coalition of governors from both parties legalising starter homes.
- Let account money be used for a first-home down payment.

Be honest about the tension. Building more homes helps the young and future buyers by slowing price growth, which means existing owners' paper wealth grows more slowly. That is a real transfer from one group to another, and it is why this is a supporting plank rather than the centrepiece.

**Why this order.**
- **Pillar 1 comes first** because the interests line up now and capital is low. You bank a win instead of picking a fight.
- **The top-end tax goes in as Pillar 1's pay-for, not as a stand-alone fight.** "Millionaires pay so every child owns something" is a fairness story that keeps the program alive. A tax increase on its own is just a fight.
- **The hardest top-end changes** (the payroll tax cap and step-up reform) **wait for the 2032 deadline,** when leaving things as they are stops being an option for anyone. That is Thatcher's lesson: don't fight until you can outlast the other side.
- **Building the PROMISE machinery now** means a drafted, scored plan is on the table when that window opens. When a window opens, the side with a ready plan usually wins.

### Narrative for each audience

**Discipline: never say "wealth gap" from the podium.** Because voters now dislike the other party more than they like their own, the phrase is coded as one side's. Say "ownership" and "same rules."
- **The President's base:** "Every American child a shareholder in America. Ownership, not handouts. And Wall Street's tricks shouldn't make a fund manager's tax rate lower than a nurse's."
- **Democrats:** "Baby bonds, paid for by millionaires. We made Trump's accounts work for working families."
- **Independents:** "Every kid starts with a stake. Everyone at the top plays by the same rules."
- **Seniors:** "Social Security protected, never privatised. It's in the law."
- **Markets and CBO:** "Fully paid for. No new borrowing."

### Building the coalition

The strongest messengers are the unusual ones: philanthropists and investors who are already funding accounts, CEOs whose companies contribute, Connecticut's state treasurer, Republican senators sponsoring auto-enrolment and expansion, Democratic baby-bonds authors, AARP on the firewall, and the asset managers. Hold one joint event at the start and no partisan events on this issue afterwards.

### Handling the losers

- **Very high earners:** a threshold indexed to inflation, phased in, and no wealth tax.
- **Private equity:** grandfather existing funds, or trade carried interest away for a lower bracket threshold.
- **Farm and family-business lobby:** postpone step-up reform to Phase 2, then offer a high exemption plus deferral while the business keeps operating.
- **Existing homeowners:** focus new supply on underused commercial land and transit corridors, not on rezoning established neighbourhoods by decree.

## 8. Delivery Architecture

**Owner.** The Treasury Secretary, with an **Ownership Delivery Unit** of 10–20 people under a Deputy Secretary. It holds weekly stocktakes and a monthly 30-minute review with the President, tracking take-up by income band. This is Michael Barber's UK Delivery Unit model: a few priorities, hard data, a routine, and the leader's attention.

**Who does what on Monday morning.**

A baby is born in Toledo. At the hospital, the parents request a Social Security number through Enumeration at Birth. SSA forwards the record to Treasury. The Bureau of the Fiscal Service opens the account in the common trust and deposits the seed within days. The parents receive a letter and an app link. They have to claim the account only if they want to add family money; public deposits arrive regardless.

Each spring, the IRS flags children eligible for the low-income top-up from the prior year's tax returns. State Medicaid and SNAP data cover the families who don't file. Fiscal Service makes the deposits.

At 18, the account becomes the young adult's own IRA. From then on, Saver's Match deposits and any contributions through an employer or a state auto-IRA land in the same place.

**The capacity gaps, and how to close them.**
- **IRS staffing.** Ring-fence delivery and anti-fraud staff for Saver's Match and the top-up in the next appropriations bill. Present it as "the staff that run the President's programs," which is accurate.
- **Data-sharing.** Agreements between SSA and Treasury (already in hand under one official), and between the states and Treasury for Medicaid and SNAP data. Fund the states to send it.
- **Identity checks** for parents claiming accounts. This is where the Permanent Secretary and the Citizen at the Counter disagree. The Permanent Secretary wants strong identity checks to prevent fraud. The Citizen at the Counter knows every extra step kills take-up among exactly the families the program is for. The resolution: public deposits need no claim, and identity checks apply only when private money moves in or any money moves out.
- **Improper payments.** Run Saver's Match's first year (2028) as a closely monitored launch with a contingency engineering team on standby. HealthCare.gov's 2013 collapse is the cautionary tale.

**Money.** The deposits are mandatory spending set by formula, not annual appropriations. IRS and SSA delivery staff come through appropriations, protected by naming them in statute.

**Legal instruments.** A statute (the Ownership Act and the PROMISE Act); Treasury regulations to cover the gap until the statute passes; appropriations; data agreements between agencies and with states.

**From pilot to national scale.** The seed is already national. The top-up grows one birth cohort at a time, which is a natural pilot. Expect a stabilisation period of 6–12 months after any big IT launch, and budget for it. Big federal IT projects reliably start badly; plan for that instead of hoping.

## 10. Pre-Mortem and Red Team

### How this fails: four stories from 2031

**1. The 529 outcome.** The pay-for collapsed in 2027 and the top-up was dropped to save the bill. Auto-enrolment stayed in regulations and was never written into law. Accounts filled fastest in families who could contribute $5,000 a year. By 2031 the gap in balances between richer and poorer families looked like the 529 gap, and critics who called it "a tax shelter for the comfortable" were right.
*Prevention:* make the formula the thing you never trade. Trade the name, the threshold and the size of the top-up instead.

**2. The poisoned brand.** Democrats refused to improve a "Trump" program before 2028. The seed lapsed at the end of the pilot. A new administration let outreach wither, and the accounts became the American Child Trust Fund: opened, forgotten, unclaimed.
*Prevention:* pass in early 2027 with two-party names, a mandatory permanent seed, and the low-income top-up branded as the Democrats'.

**3. The delivery scandal.** Saver's Match's first payments in 2028, run on a hollowed-out IRS, went to the wrong accounts and drew fraud. An inspector general's report made "government can't run accounts" the story, and Congress froze the expansion.
*Prevention:* fund delivery staff, use one set of payment rails rather than five, pilot the hard parts, and set tripwires (section 12).

**4. The regressive cliff deal.** In 2032, Congress closed the Social Security gap mostly through benefit cuts and a higher retirement age. The bottom half lost a fifth of its largest asset in slow motion.
*Prevention:* get a progressive base bill drafted through the Advisory Board now; build the coalition of AARP, unions and the ownership groups; make the firewall the price of support.

### The opposition's strongest case

**From the left.** "This lets a regressive law off the hook. The 2025 reconciliation law took about $1,200 a year from the bottom tenth and gave about $13,600 to the top tenth. A millionaire bracket and $1,000 deposits are pocket change against that. It entrenches a privatised, stocks-only model of welfare and hands an unpopular president a legacy."

The honest answer: much of that is true. The Compact does not reverse the 2025 law's distribution, and nothing that requires this President's signature will. What it does is build lasting infrastructure and a constituency while the window is open, and position the bigger fights (Medicaid, Social Security financing, step-up reform) for 2029–32. If the goal is to punish the 2025 law rather than build ownership, this is the wrong strategy. That is a values choice for the principal.

**From the right.** "Government deposits are welfare dressed as capitalism; tax increases hit job creators; the deficit is already a crisis; growth, not redistribution, lifts all boats."

The answer: Chetty's work shows growth alone does not restore mobility, while broadly shared growth does. The package is fully paid for. It is capital, not consumption. And the right's own leaders built it: Cruz drafted the original, Capito, Justice and Moreno want it automatic and expanded, and the President's name is on it.

### Hirschman's three attacks

The economist Albert Hirschman showed that reforms are attacked in three standard ways: it will backfire, it won't change anything, or it will endanger something precious.
- **It will backfire (perversity): "the rich will use these accounts more."** True of the current design. The answer is the progressive top-up, automatic seeding and a fixed contribution cap.
- **It won't change anything (futility): "$1,000 can't dent trillions."** Correct about the aggregate top share within a decade. Wrong about the target. The goal is ownership and the wealth of young adults, where modelling shows large effects: Morningstar estimates baby bonds would roughly halve the Black–white gap in resources per child at 18 ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/economy/can-baby-bonds-shrink-racial-wealth-gap)).
- **It will endanger something precious (jeopardy): "this is a Trojan horse to privatise Social Security."** A real fear, given the Bush history and the proposals to extend accounts to adults under 40. The answer is the firewall in statute, and making support conditional on it.

## 11. Durability

The design aims to make the Compact someone else's interest to defend.

- **Property, not a program.** A balance in a child's name is far harder to take back than a benefit that simply stops. A future Congress could stop new deposits but would struggle to seize existing balances.
- **Constituencies it creates:**
  - parents of tens of millions of children;
  - more than 50 employers who have pledged contributions;
  - philanthropists with billions already committed;
  - asset managers with assets to protect;
  - states that deposit their own money (Connecticut-style programs can deposit directly, and government contributions are outside the cap);
  - eventually, the young adults themselves.
- **Mandatory spending, set by formula and indexed,** so there is no annual appropriation fight like the one that shrank Alaska's dividend.
- **In statute, not regulation,** so a new administration cannot reverse it by rewriting rules and courts cannot strike it down for lack of authority.
- **Two parents.** The account carries a Republican name and the top-up a Democratic one, so neither party can kill it without killing its own work. If a future government renames the program, that is a cosmetic cost, and Progresa shows a rename can be survived.
- **Visible.** Annual statements turn a hidden tax-code benefit into something families can see and defend.
- **Evidence.** Independent evaluation makes a quiet repeal costly.

**Surviving a change of government.** The weakest moment is January 2029. The defences are a statute already law for two years, deposits already flowing, statements already in kitchens, and the next administration's own party's names on part of it.

- **The data:** the Fed DFA, the 2025 SCF, the 2026 Trustees report, and the CBO baseline.
- **The legal facts:** Trump Accounts law and Treasury's auto-enrolment rules; the Saver's Match timeline; the PROMISE Act text as described by its sponsors.
- **The live political signals:** the President's 2025 openness to a 39.6% bracket and to taxing carried interest ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/08/trump-taxes-millionaires-medicaid)); Republican pushes to make Trump Accounts automatic and to expand them ([Fox Business](https://foxbusiness.com/politics/gop-senator-unveils-plan-expand-trump-accounts-people-under-40-game-changer)).

**What we assume.**
- Divided government from January, going by AP's count.
- The President values permanence and legacy enough to accept a progressive formula and a top-end pay-for.
- Democratic leaders value a delivered win more than denying one.
- CBO scores land near the estimates above.

**What is judgment.**
- Every probability in this dossier.
- The judgment that Passes is the binding gate for Pillar 1, and Lasts for everything else.

**Deliberately left out of Phase 1.**
- **Reversing the 2025 law's Medicaid and SNAP cuts.** These matter a great deal for the bottom half's balance sheets, but they cannot be won with this principal's signature.
- **A wealth tax.**
- **Race-specific programs.**
- **Antitrust and labour-market reforms that address concentration.**

All four belong to a different strategy, with a different principal and a different window.

**What would change the recommendation.**
- **The President refuses any top-end pay-for.** Pivot to the lite bill and the PROMISE Act, and hold the progressive top-up for 2029.
- **A recession hits.** That opens a crisis window: cash support through IRS payment systems (option B), with a share deposited into accounts.
- **The principal's goal is the ceiling, not the floor.** Rebuild the strategy around concentrated power: political-finance rules, competition policy, estate and gift reform, and preparing for a one-party government.
- **New evidence shows bottom-half decline is mainly driven by debt.** Raise relief from medical and other consumer debt to a full pillar.

---

## 15. The Statecraft Lesson

When your opponents build the rails, don't fight the rails; fight over what runs on them. America's most durable social policies are the ones both tribes can claim. The EITC survived half a century because one party could call it pro-work and the other anti-poverty. Bismarck built social insurance to beat the socialists at their own game. Medicare's prescription-drug benefit was passed by Republicans and defended by Democrats. The wealth gap won't be closed by the side that wins the argument about inequality. It will be narrowed by whoever builds the machine that makes ownership automatic and then makes it someone else's interest to defend. The question to sit with: would you rather win with the other side's name on it, or lose with yours?
