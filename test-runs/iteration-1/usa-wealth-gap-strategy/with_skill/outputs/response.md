Here's what I'd tell the President. The wealth gap as it's usually measured can't be "fixed" in one term by anything that can actually pass. And the fix most people reach for first, a tax on billionaires' wealth, is the one most likely to fail. What can be done is to change who owns America: make every child an owner automatically, protect the bottom half's biggest asset from the 2032 Social Security cliff, and pay for both by making the very top play by the same rules as everyone else. The useful surprise is that the machinery for the first part already exists, and it carries this President's name.

I've written the full plan up as a strategy dossier (`dossier-usa-wealth-gap-2026-11-25.md`), covering the power map, delivery design, wargame, pre-mortem, dashboard and first 100 days. Here's the core of it and the reasoning behind it.

## First, which gap?

"The wealth gap" is really four problems, and each needs different tools and faces different opponents.

**1. Concentration at the top.** The top 1% held 32.5% of household wealth in the second quarter of 2026, the highest share since the Fed's records begin in 1989 ([Fed data via 24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/)).

**2. The bottom half owns almost nothing.** It holds 2.3% of household wealth, less than the 3.5% it held in 1989.

**3. The racial gap.** Black household net worth fell 25% between 2022 and 2025 ([theGrio on the 2025 SCF](https://thegrio.com/2026/10/14/black-wealth-drops-25-trumps-agenda-is-making-the-squeeze-worse/)).

**4. The generational squeeze.** The median first-time homebuyer is now 40, a record ([NAR via Inman](https://www.inman.com/2026/11/05/first-time-homebuyer-data-nar/)).

Here's where I'd push back on how the problem is usually framed. If you define success as shrinking the top 1%'s share, you've chosen a number that mostly tracks the stock market. The top 1% own about 51% of the stocks and funds that households hold outside retirement accounts. The bottom half owns houses, cars and cash. In the second quarter of 2026 alone, household stock holdings rose $10.7 trillion ([Fed Z.1](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/20260911/recent_developments.htm)). That is more than double everything the bottom half owns, which is roughly $4–4.5 trillion. A market crash would "fix" that target faster than any law could, and a boom will swamp whatever you pass. So track the top share, but set your targets at the bottom.

The second correction matters even more. The official wealth figures leave out Social Security, which is the bottom half's largest asset. Wharton's Sylvain Catherine and his co-authors found that the top 1%'s share rose 7.6 points between 1989 and 2019 in the Fed's data. Once Social Security wealth is counted, the rise is only 1.5 points ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/social-security-reduced-wealth-inequality-could-be-insolvent-2032/)). The 2026 Trustees report projects that the retirement trust fund runs dry in late 2032, which triggers an automatic 22% benefit cut ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/06/09/social-security-trust-fund-medicare)). In other words, the biggest scheduled widening of the wealth gap in the next decade isn't a tax cut or a market boom. It's that cliff.

So the version worth attacking is the second one: the bottom half owning almost nothing. A design aimed at that also narrows the racial and generational gaps, without needing race-based eligibility rules, which would invite lawsuits.

## The seat and the clock

The principal is the sitting President. He has 26 months left and can't run again.

As of Nov 25, 10:35am GMT+8, AP's count shows Democrats with 259 House seats won and Republicans with 173. Three House seats are counted as won for neither party in the AP results I have, and those results don't say why or which way they'll go. In the Senate, AP's count at the same time shows Democrats with a total of 56 (including two others who caucus with them) and Republicans with a total of 44. These numbers may have changed since then. The label "Majority reached" beside the Democrats in the House is not a call by AP, and neither is "Control reached" beside them in the Senate. Going by AP's count, I'm planning for divided government from January. Treat that as a working assumption, not the only possible outcome.

What decides the strategy is structure, not seat margins:
- **Every bill needs the President's signature**, or a two-thirds override in both chambers.
- **Most bills need 60 votes in the Senate.** The filibuster survived the push to abolish it this year ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/08/03/the-not-so-secret-filibuster-society/)).
- **His political capital is thin.** Post-midterm approval is 32% in both Economist/YouGov and Reuters/Ipsos polling ([Newsweek](https://newsweek.com/donald-trump-approval-rating-falling-midterm-losses-12709719)).
- **Fiscal room is tight.** Federal debt is projected to rise from 99% of GDP to 120% by 2036 ([CRFB on CBO](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cbo-releases-february-2026-budget-and-economic-outlook)).

Put together, this means one thing. Anything that passes in 2027–28 passes as a bipartisan deal, it has to be paid for, and it has to be something the President actually wants.

That points to the binding constraint. A policy has to get through four gates: it has to work, pass, be delivered and last. Most plans to "fix the wealth gap" die at the second gate, getting passed. The few that pass usually die at the fourth, lasting. The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit cut child poverty to a record low, then expired because it was temporary and only one party owned it. So the strategy has to be built around those two gates. Clever mechanism design alone won't save it.

## The strategy: three moves, in this order

**1. The Ownership Act (2027): make Trump Accounts automatic, permanent and progressive.**

The 2025 law already gives children invested accounts. A temporary rule now lets Treasury open them automatically for about 73 million kids ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/trump-accounts-treasury-automatic-child-enrollment.amp)). But as built, the program will widen gaps rather than close them, for three reasons:
- **The $1,000 seed requires an opt-in.** By mid-June only about 39% of eligible children had claimed it ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/trump-accounts-surpass-6-million-but-signs-of-a-wealth-gap-stoke-concerns/267118)).
- **The seed is flat.** Every eligible child gets the same amount, however poor the family.
- **It rewards families who can already save.** Families can put in up to $5,000 a year, which reproduces the pattern of 529 college-savings plans. Fewer than 1% of lower-income families with young children own one, against nearly three in ten of the highest-income families. A typical account with just the seed reaches about $4,000 by adulthood ([Hamilton in Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2026/07/16/trump-accounts-rich-families-529-data/)).

The fix:
- **Open the account automatically at birth**, when parents request the baby's Social Security number in the hospital. Senators Capito and Justice have already proposed exactly this.
- **Deposit the seed automatically, and make it permanent.** That costs about $3.6 billion a year, based on 2025's 3.6 million births.
- **Add an "Opportunity Deposit"** of up to $1,000 a year for children in families below the poverty line, phasing out at three times the poverty line. Scaled from CRFB's scoring of the baby-bonds plan, that is roughly $100–125 billion over the first decade ([CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cory-bookers-baby-bonds-plan)).
- **Make it one account for life.** At 18 it becomes the young adult's own retirement account, and the new Saver's Match keeps adding to it.

The result: a child from a poor family reaches 18 with roughly $25,000–30,000 in today's money, assuming real returns of 3–5% a year. Without the deposit, that child would have about $4,000.

Pay for it with a 39.6% tax rate on income above $1 million, which Penn Wharton scores at $222 billion over ten years. The President floated a version of this in 2025, reportedly only above $2.5 million, which raises just $22 billion ([Penn Wharton](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/p/2025-05-05-raising-top-ordinary-rates-options-for-reform-under-tcja-extension/); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/08/trump-taxes-millionaires-medicaid)). That threshold is the real negotiation.

Each side has its own reason to sign:
- **The President** gets his signature program made permanent, a legacy as the president who "made every child an owner," and a signing ceremony.
- **Democrats** get their own baby-bonds formula, paid for by millionaires.

**2. Protect the floor.** Sign the bipartisan PROMISE Act now. It sets up a fast-track that forces Congress to vote on Social Security solvency before the 2032 cut ([Sen. Durbin](https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/durbin-cassidy-kaine-tillis-king-cornyn-coons-armstrong-introduce-bipartisan-proposal-to-initiate-congressional-action-on-social-security)). Set the President's terms early:
- **A written firewall.** The accounts add to Social Security and never carve money out of it. That carve-out is what killed George W. Bush's 2005 personal-accounts plan.
- **A big share of the fix from the top.** Applying the payroll tax to earnings above $400,000 closes about half of the 75-year shortfall, according to Social Security's own actuaries ([SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/solvency/provisions/payrolltax_summary.html)).

Then make sure the Saver's Match works when its first deposits arrive in 2028, with a default account for anyone who doesn't have one.

**3. Same rules at the top.** In 2027, the top-end measures serve as the pay-for: the millionaire rate, ending the carried-interest break that lets fund managers pay capital-gains rates, and restoring IRS audits of the very wealthy and of partnerships. Partnership audits fell 76% after the 2025 staff cuts.

The harder fight comes in 2029–32, when the Social Security cliff forces a deal. That's when to close "buy, borrow, die": the strategy of borrowing against appreciated assets instead of selling them, then passing them on at death. Under current rules, those gains are wiped clean at death ("step-up in basis"), which costs about $72.5 billion a year.

What I wouldn't do is spend capital on a wealth tax. News outlets including KTVU report that California voters rejected even a one-time billionaire tax this month ([KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/prop-40-californias-billionaire-tax-fails-ballot-box)). The IRS has lost more than a quarter of its enforcement and collection staff. And the courts left the constitutional question open in *Moore* (2024).

**Why this order.** Start with the move where the other side's interests already line up with yours, while political capital is low. Use the fairness story ("millionaires pay so every kid owns something") to protect it. Hold the hardest fight for the moment when the 2032 deadline makes doing nothing unacceptable to everyone, which is how the 1983 Social Security rescue happened.

## How we make sure it actually works

Think of it in three layers.

**Delivery.** One set of payment rails, automatic, with no forms. Picture a baby born in Toledo on a Monday. The parents request a Social Security number at the hospital. Social Security sends the record to Treasury, and the account opens and is funded within days. Delivery has an unusual advantage: one official, Frank Bisignano, currently runs both the IRS and Social Security. The weak point is IRS capacity. More than 25,000 staff left in 2025 ([OPB/NPR](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/03/more-taxes-have-gone-uncollected-after-irs-slashed-its-staff/)). So the President has to reverse the IRS cuts his own budget proposes for 2027, because those are the people who will run his programs.

**Durability.** The design makes the program someone else's interest to defend:
- **Property, not a program.** A balance in a child's name is far harder to claw back than a benefit to cancel.
- **A built-in defending coalition:** parents, more than 50 employers already contributing, the Dells' $6.25 billion pledge, and the asset managers who hold the money.
- **Locked in law.** Mandatory spending, in statute rather than regulation.
- **Two parents.** The account carries his name and the deposit carries the Democrats'.
- **Visible.** Annual statements, so families can see the benefit and will fight for it.

The cautionary tale is Britain's Child Trust Fund, which was abolished in the first austerity round precisely because nobody felt they owned it.

**Feedback.**
- **Targets to hit:**
  - seed take-up above 95% by 2028;
  - no automatic Social Security cut in 2032;
  - retirement-plan participation up from 65% to 75% by 2034;
  - the bottom half's wealth share up from 2.3% to at least 3.0% by 2036.
- **Track, don't target:** the top 1%'s share.
- **Tripwires, with responses agreed in advance:**
  - If seed take-up is below 70% by December 2027, force automatic election onto the next must-pass bill.
  - If Saver's Match improper payments exceed 5%, pause and fix before expanding.
  - If anyone attaches a Social Security carve-out, walk away.

**Odds line for the Ownership Act.** Works 0.6, Passes 0.4, Delivered 0.7, Lasts 0.7. Multiplied together, that comes to about 12%. The binding gate is Passes. That number sounds low, and it's the honest one. For comparison, a stand-alone baby-bonds bill in this Congress would be about 0.6%, and a wealth tax about 0.1%. Across all three moves, the chance of at least one durable, meaningful win by 2032 is roughly one in three.

## The uncomfortable part

This won't shrink the top 1%'s share much within a decade. Nothing that can pass will.

It won't undo the 2025 law either. CBO estimates that law costs the poorest tenth of households about $1,200 a year while the richest tenth gain about $13,600 ([Thomson Reuters on CBO](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/cbo-analyzes-distributional-effects-of-budget-act/)). If reversing that is the real goal, this is the wrong strategy and the wrong seat.

It also asks you to hand a president at 32% approval a win with his name on it. If that makes you recoil, notice the reflex: that instinct, applied by voters and politicians on both sides, is exactly what kills durable American policy.

And building more homes, the other big lever for the young, means existing owners' wealth grows more slowly. That's a real trade-off, not a free lunch.

If you meant a different president, say a new one in January 2029 whose party holds both chambers, the plan shifts. Reconciliation opens up for the deposit and for ending step-up, and the binding gate moves from Passes to Lasts. I can rerun it from that seat.

**The question I'd put to you:** which gap do you actually want closed, the floor (everyone owns something real) or the ceiling (no one owns too much of the country)? They're different missions with different enemies. Everything above is built for the floor. If it's really the ceiling you care about, because concentrated wealth becomes concentrated political power, the strategy changes. It becomes a longer fight over money in politics, competition policy and estate taxes, and I'd rebuild it around that.

**Statecraft lesson.** When your opponents build the rails, don't fight the rails; fight over what runs on them. The Earned Income Tax Credit has survived half a century of partisan war because Republicans could call it pro-work and Democrats could call it anti-poverty. Bismarck built social insurance to beat the socialists at their own game. The side that wins the argument about inequality won't be the one that narrows the wealth gap. That will be whoever builds the machine that makes ownership automatic, and then makes it someone else's interest to defend. Worth sitting with: would you rather win with the other side's name on it, or lose with yours?
