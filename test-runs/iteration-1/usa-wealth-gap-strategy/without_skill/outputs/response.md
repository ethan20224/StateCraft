# Fixing America's Wealth Gap: The Strategy I'd Put on the President's Desk

**My assumptions (I couldn't ask you):** By "wealth gap" I mean how net worth is spread out (what households own minus what they owe), not income. I'm assuming a president who will spend real political capital on this, a Congress with thin margins (the normal American situation), and a meaning of "actually fix it" that is lasting, measurable change over a generation rather than a bill-signing ceremony. I've written the plan so a president of either party could run it. As you'll see, that isn't politeness. It's the center of the strategy.

---

## The five-minute version

If I had five minutes with the President, I'd say three things.

**First, "the wealth gap" is really two problems with one name.** Most of the last forty years of failure comes from treating them as one. Problem one: half the country owns almost nothing that grows over time. Problem two: wealth at the very top grows faster than everything else and is taxed more lightly than a paycheck, sometimes not at all. Taxing the top doesn't give anyone at the bottom an asset. Giving the bottom assets doesn't slow the top's growth. You need both moves, and you need them connected, so that money raised by closing loopholes at the top pays for ownership at the bottom.

**Second, the hard part isn't ideas. It's making them last.** Wealth builds up slowly and takes a generation to shift. A presidency lasts four to eight years. Every serious piece of this has to survive the next president from the other party. If it gets branded as one side's trophy, it gets repealed.

**Third, and this is the part almost nobody has noticed: the last four years built most of the pipes by accident.** There's a bipartisan federal retirement match from 2022 that applies to contributions starting this January. There's an investment account for every American child from a 2025 Republican law, with tens of millions of kids already enrolled. And this summer brought the biggest bipartisan housing law in decades. The strategy is to connect, fund and fix what already exists, not to invent something new that one party owns.

The rest of this memo works through the problem in layers: the numbers, why the gap widens on its own, what "fixed" should mean, the plan itself, how to actually carry it out, and how to know whether it's working.

---

## 1. What we're up against

According to the Federal Reserve's distributional accounts for Q2 2026, the [top 1% of households now hold 32.5% of U.S. household wealth](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/). That's the highest share since the series began in 1989. The top 0.1% alone hold 15%, and the bottom half of all households hold 2.3%. Total household net worth was [$195.9 trillion at the end of Q2 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/20260911/recent_developments.htm). Do the arithmetic and roughly 1.3 million households own about $64 trillion, while the roughly 66 million households in the bottom half own about $4.5 trillion between them. The top 1% holds about fourteen times what the entire bottom half holds combined.

The history matters more than the snapshot. The bottom half held 3.5% in 1989 and peaked at 4.1% in 1992. It then fell to **0.4%** in several quarters of 2010–2012, when the housing crash wiped out their home equity, much of it borrowed against. It recovered to 2.7% in 2022 and has slipped back to 2.3% ([same source](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/)). Remember that 2010 collapse. It's the most important design lesson in this whole memo.

The racial gap sits on top of all this. In the Fed's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, the median white family had [$285,010 in wealth, the median Black family $44,890, and the median Hispanic family $61,620](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/greater-wealth-greater-uncertainty-changes-in-racial-inequality-in-the-survey-of-consumer-finances-20231018.html). That means a typical Black family holds about 16 cents for every dollar a typical white family holds. Homeownership rates were 73% for white families, 46% for Black families and 51% for Hispanic families. The racial gap in income is large. The gap in wealth is several times larger, and that alone tells you wealth is passed down through something other than paychecks.

## 2. Why the gap widens on its own

Before choosing tools, you have to understand what produces the gap. Four forces matter most.

**Asset prices.** In Q2 2026, household wealth rose $12.8 trillion in a single quarter. [$10.7 trillion of that came from stocks and $1.1 trillion from owner-occupied homes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/20260911/recent_developments.htm). The top 1% own [50.9% of the stocks and mutual funds held outside retirement plans](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/), while the bottom half's wealth is mostly homes with large mortgages. So when stocks rise, the gap widens automatically, without anyone doing anything wrong. When housing crashes, the bottom half gets wiped out, because their one asset was bought mostly with borrowed money. The bottom half's problem isn't simply "not enough money." They don't own assets that grow, and the one asset they do own carries too much debt.

**No starting stake.** Investment growth needs something to start from. Wealthier families pass down stakes: help with a down payment, tuition already paid, a cushion that lets a 25-year-old take a risk such as starting a company. A kid who starts at zero spends their twenties paying off debt instead of building wealth. That's why the racial wealth gap is so much wider than the income gap. Inheritance carries what wages don't.

**The tax code treats investment gains differently from wages.** Wages are taxed every year, withheld from the paycheck and reported to the IRS, and they show [compliance above 99%](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/weakened-irs-has-substantial-consequences). Investment gains are taxed only when you sell. If you hold an asset until death, the "step-up in basis" rule erases the gain for your heirs permanently, so it's never taxed. This produces the well-known strategy for very large fortunes, sometimes called "buy, borrow, die": buy assets, borrow against them to pay for your life (loans aren't income), and die, after which your heirs inherit with the gains wiped clean. On top of that, the 2025 tax law set the estate tax exemption at [$15 million per person ($30 million per couple), permanent and indexed to inflation](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/08/estate-tax-alert-new-15-million-federal-exemption-becomes-law).

Enforcement follows the same pattern. Unpaid tax is concentrated in income the IRS can't check automatically. The Yale Budget Lab estimates [57% misreporting on sole-proprietor income and 12% on partnership and S-corp income](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/weakened-irs-has-substantial-consequences). Audit rates for people earning over $5 million fell from about 16% in 2010 to about 2% in 2019, even though an hour spent auditing someone earning over $5 million produces about $4,900 in recommended tax, against about $650 for someone earning around $200,000. Then came 2025. IRS enforcement and collection staff fell by 27%, [audit revenue fell 35% (from $10 billion to $6.5 billion), and partnership audits fell 76% between 2023 and 2025](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/03/more-taxes-have-gone-uncollected-after-irs-slashed-its-staff/).

**Not enough housing.** White House economists estimate the country is [short 10 million homes, and home prices have risen 82% since 2000 while incomes rose 12%](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/the-u-s-is-short-10-million-houses-a-new-white-house-report-lays-out-a-blueprint-to-fix-that). A home is the middle class's main way of building wealth. When homes are scarce, wealth effectively moves from renters and young people to people who already own.

## 3. Define "fixed" before you spend a dollar

This is the assumption I'd push back on hardest, including the one built into your question. "Fix the wealth gap" sounds like it means "shrink the top 1%'s share." If that's the scoreboard, you will lose, or look like you're losing, for reasons that have nothing to do with policy. A strong stock market can push the top share up in the same year that bottom-half families get noticeably richer. A crash can shrink the top share while making everyone poorer, and the 2010 numbers show the bottom half gets hurt most. A president who steers by that share is really steering by the S&P 500.

So the scorecard should track things policy actually controls. The top share still gets reported, but as background, not as the thing you steer by. The targets below are illustrative, but they're the kind that can be written into law in advance and checked by someone independent:

| What we measure | Why it matters | Illustrative target |
|---|---|---|
| Median account balance at 18 for kids from families below median income | Whether every young adult starts with a stake | At least $25,000 (in 2026 dollars) for the group turning 18 in 2045 |
| Share of bottom-half workers with a retirement account that's receiving contributions | Ownership of assets that grow | About 80% within 10 years |
| Median net worth of bottom-half households, after inflation | Real progress that doesn't depend on stock-market swings | Double by 2045 |
| New housing permits relative to need in expensive metros; homeownership under age 35 | Access to the middle class's main asset | Close half the 10-million-home shortfall within 10 years |
| Effective tax rate of the top 0.1% (counting unrealized gains) compared with the median wage earner | Whether the rules at the top match everyone else's | Published every year, with equal rates as the goal |
| Black–white and Hispanic–white median wealth ratio among adults aged 18–35 | The racial gap in the next generation | Cut in half by 2045 |
| Top 1% share vs. bottom 50% share | Background | Reported, not targeted |

## 4. The plan: three pillars and one loop

### Pillar 1: Make every American an owner automatically

Singapore is the clearest proof that ownership can be designed on purpose. In 2025, [91.2% of its resident households owned their homes](https://www.singstat.gov.sg/find-data/explore-data-themes/households/resident-households/latest-news-data). It got there through compulsory savings (the CPF system takes up to about 37% of a younger worker's pay, split between employer and employee) that flow into state-built housing on land the state mostly owns. The U.S. can't copy that. It won't mandate a 37% savings rate or nationalize land. What does carry over is the principle: **ownership came from automatic contributions flowing into a specific asset that grows, not from taxing the rich.** Everything in Pillar 1 applies that principle at American scale.

**1a. Turn Trump Accounts into real "baby bonds."** (Baby bonds are publicly funded investment accounts given to children at birth.) The 2025 law created investment accounts for children. Kids born from 2025 through 2028 get a [$1,000 Treasury deposit, and the Dell Foundation is adding $250 for up to 25 million older children in ZIP codes with median family incomes up to $118,000](https://ctmirror.org/2026/10/07/trump-accounts-auto-enrolled/). Families can add up to $5,000 a year and employers up to $2,500. The money goes into U.S. stock index funds and stays locked until age 18. The White House estimates more than 60 million children were enrolled automatically. That's the hardest part of any baby-bond program, the account system itself, and it's already built.

The problem is that, as designed, it mostly works as a tax-advantaged account, and families with spare cash fill those to the limit. The White House's own projection has the $1,000 deposit growing to about $6,000 by age 18. Fidelity's example of a fully funded account reaches [nearly $200,000](https://ctmirror.org/2026/10/07/trump-accounts-auto-enrolled/). That's a roughly 33-to-1 gap at 18 inside the same program. Left alone, Trump Accounts could widen the next generation's wealth gap instead of narrowing it. That's not a knock on the idea. A piece is missing.

There's a second trap: the $1,000 deposit arrives **only once a parent or guardian claims the account**, through a mobile app. Against more than 60 million automatically enrolled kids, parents had set up accounts for about 8 million children by October ([same source](https://ctmirror.org/2026/10/07/trump-accounts-auto-enrolled/)). When a program needs people to opt in, the families who most need it are the ones who miss out.

The fix has four parts. First, make the $1,000 starting deposit permanent rather than limited to 2025–2028 births, and deposit it automatically with no claim step. Second, add a **public deposit every year that's larger for poorer families**: for example, $1,000 a year for children in families below median income, shrinking to zero above that. Assuming a 4% return after inflation, that plus the starting deposit comes to roughly $28,000 in today's money at age 18 (my estimate). With roughly 35 million eligible children, it costs about $30–40 billion a year, around 0.1% of GDP. Third, exclude these balances from the asset limits in benefit programs. SSI, for example, cuts off people with more than $2,000 in assets, and that rule punishes exactly the saving we want. Fourth, cap fees and make a low-cost index fund the default.

The evidence so far is promising but early. Connecticut's state program puts [$3,200 into an investment for every baby whose birth is covered by the state's Medicaid program, has enrolled more than 33,000 children, and projects $11,000 to $24,000 by ages 18–30](https://portal.ct.gov/ott/newsroom/news/news-releases/ct-baby-bonds-turns-two-over-33000-children-now-eligible). In one simulation of a universal program that gives more to poorer families, economist Naomi Zewde found the median white-to-Black wealth ratio among young adults could fall [from about 15.8:1 to about 1.4:1](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/baby-bonds-would-reduce-racial-wealth-inequities-heres-what-policymakers-need-know). Be honest about what that is. It's a simulation, not a result anyone has seen. Connecticut's first group doesn't turn 18 until 2041.

One design rule is essential: **eligibility based on income and wealth, not race.** Programs that sort people by race face the strictest level of court review. The 2021 farmer debt relief that was limited by race was blocked in court and later repealed. Because income and wealth track closely with race in the U.S., a program that gives more to poorer families captures much of the racial effect, and it holds up in court. Reparations advocates argue, seriously, that race-neutral programs can't fully close a gap that race-specific policy created. That's a real argument. But a program stuck in court for ten years closes nothing.

And **keep the name.** Renaming Trump Accounts would start a partisan fight that puts the money at risk. Once tens of millions of families are watching their children's balances grow, the accounts become what Social Security is: too widely held for any politician to touch.

**1b. Make the Saver's Match automatic.** Starting January 1, 2027, a provision of the 2022 SECURE 2.0 law puts a federal match into the retirement accounts of lower- and middle-income workers: [50% of up to $2,000 in contributions, so up to $1,000 a year. It phases out between $41,000 and $71,000 of income for married couples and between $20,500 and $35,500 for single filers](https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/insights/2026/09/guidance-issued-on-upcoming-savers-match-for-retirement-plan-contributions). The first payments go out in 2028 for 2027 contributions, and a [TrumpIRA.gov site launching January 1, 2027 will list low-cost IRAs that accept the match](https://401kspecialistmag.com/treasury-irs-begin-rulemaking-for-2027-savers-match-advance-trumpira-gov/). One industry estimate says it [could help 21.9 million Americans](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/secure-20-savers-match-could-help-219m-americans/250294).

The catch: a worker must file a tax return and claim the match on a specific form (8880-A), must have an account to receive it, and employer plans must choose to accept the payments ([WTW](https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/insights/2026/09/guidance-issued-on-upcoming-savers-match-for-retirement-plan-contributions)). Each of those steps is a point where low-income workers drop out. The fix is a **public default account**. Either open the federal Thrift Savings Plan (the low-cost retirement plan federal employees already use) to every worker, or create a Treasury-run default IRA. Then the match lands somewhere even if the worker never picks a provider, with the claim pre-filled from W-2 data.

Pair that with a **federal automatic IRA requirement** for employers that offer no retirement plan: workers are signed up automatically and can opt out. Fifteen states already run these programs, with [more than 1 million workers who have saved over $2.5 billion](https://www.pew.org/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/02/04/status-of-state-auto-ira-savings-programs). Make it national, with tax credits to cover small businesses' setup costs. It works because most people stay with whatever the default is.

**1c. Plug the leaks.** At the bottom, wealth is destroyed as often as it fails to get built: expensive credit, overdraft fees, medical debt, a car repair that forces someone to cash out a 401(k) and pay penalties. The cheapest wealth to create is wealth that never leaks out. That means scaling the emergency savings accounts that SECURE 2.0 already allows employers to link to retirement plans, and treating predatory fees as the wealth drain they are.

### Pillar 2: Unlock housing, the asset most families actually own

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law in July 2026. It [bars landlords that own 350 or more single-family homes from buying more, speeds up environmental review for building between existing structures, funds "pattern books" of pre-approved home designs, and drops the permanent-chassis requirement for manufactured homes (saving an estimated $5,000–$10,000 per home)](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/10/nx-s1-5885027/housing-bill-without-trump-signature). It also steers existing federal money toward communities that build. But it doesn't change local zoning and adds no new money. It's a first step.

The next step is making **federal money follow building permits.** The biggest lever is the multi-year highway and transit funding bill. Congress has covered that only with short-term extensions since the previous law's authority [expired on September 30, 2026](https://www.naco.org/news/federal-funding-extension-creates-transportation-funding-cliff-counties). Tie a meaningful share of road and transit money to housing permits relative to local need: metro areas that build more get more transportation funding, and areas that block construction get less. Add federal surplus land, more of the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (the main federal subsidy for building affordable rentals), and better financing for factory-built homes. Zoning is controlled by states and cities, so Washington's job is to pay for results, not order them.

There's an uncomfortable conflict built into housing policy that you need to understand. **The bottom half's wealth is mostly home equity.** A plan that crashes home prices would destroy the very wealth we're trying to protect, and the typical voter owns a home. The realistic goal is home prices that stay flat after inflation while incomes catch up over about ten years, closing the affordability gap slowly rather than through a crash. The political message follows from that: "your home keeps its value, and your kids can afford one too."

What *not* to do: subsidize buyers (down-payment grants, buyer tax credits) without adding supply. When supply is fixed, that money just raises prices. It's the most common housing mistake in Washington, and both parties make it.

The link back to Pillar 1: Trump Accounts already allow [up to $10,000 to be withdrawn without penalty for a first home](https://ctmirror.org/2026/10/07/trump-accounts-auto-enrolled/). Combined with the yearly deposits for poorer families, that gives a 20-something a real down payment, which means more equity and less debt. That's the direct answer to 2010.

### Pillar 3: Same rules at the top

The framing decides whether this survives. It isn't about punishing success. It's about a dollar of investment gain eventually being taxed like a dollar of wages. That's more accurate, and it can be defended in front of either party's voters.

**3a. End the step-up at death above a high exemption.** There are two ways to do it. The first is carryover basis: heirs keep the original purchase price, so the gain is taxed when they sell. The CBO's most recent published estimate (from 2018) put that at [about $105 billion over ten years](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/54792). The second is to treat death as a sale and tax the gain then, which raises more. Either way, add a generous exemption (say $5 million per person) and let family farms and businesses defer the tax as long as the family keeps running them.

There's a real deal available here. **Canada abolished its estate tax in 1972 and instead taxes capital gains at death.** An American version would keep the $15 million estate exemption from the 2025 law in exchange for ending the step-up. Conservatives get "no new death tax." Progressives get gains that are finally taxed once. Each side can sell that to its own voters.

**3b. Close "buy, borrow, die."** Above a very high threshold (portfolios over $100 million), treat large loans taken against assets that have gained value as a partial sale for tax purposes. This is narrow, hits a few thousand households, and targets the exact mechanism.

**3c. Rebuild IRS enforcement with guaranteed multi-year funding** that Congress can't quietly cut each year, set aside for high-income and partnership audits. The Yale Budget Lab estimates the 2025 staff cuts alone will cost [about $598 billion over 2026–2035](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/weakened-irs-has-substantial-consequences). Even if that's twice too high, reversing the cuts would bring in roughly $30 billion a year, about what the yearly children's deposits cost. CBO's more cautious estimate of the 2022 IRS funding still showed a clear net gain: about $204 billion back on $79.4 billion spent ([same source](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/weakened-irs-has-substantial-consequences)). It has to be guaranteed funding, because yearly enforcement budgets are the easiest thing in Washington to cut without anyone noticing.

**3d. What I wouldn't lead with: a federal tax on total wealth.** There are four reasons. One, the experience abroad: [twelve OECD countries had such taxes in 1990 and only four still did in 2017](https://www.oecd.org/ctp/tax-policy/role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-OECD-summary.pdf). Governments repealed them over efficiency and administrative problems and because they raised little money, and the OECD itself recommends taxing investment income and inheritances as the main tools instead. Two, legal risk: the Supreme Court's 2024 *Moore* decision deliberately left open whether Congress can tax gains that haven't been realized. Three, it targets the ratio at the top, not the lack of assets at the bottom. Four, it would use up a presidency's political capital in a ten-year court fight.

The other side deserves a fair hearing. Economists like Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman argue that a well-designed wealth tax, with few exemptions and strong enforcement, can work, and that it's the only tool that reaches billionaires who never sell. That's a serious position. My call is about order and odds: do 3a through 3c first, publish the top's effective tax rate every year, and keep a wealth tax in reserve if the gap doesn't move.

### The loop that connects them

Money raised by Pillar 3 goes into a dedicated trust fund (call it the American Ownership Fund) that pays the Pillar 1 deposits by formula. There are three reasons to set it up this way. Children's deposits shouldn't depend on yearly budget fights. The deal becomes easy to explain: "the money from closing the inheritance loophole funds your kid's account." And it creates a large group of people who will defend the revenue, because repealing the loophole fix would visibly cut their children's deposits.

## 5. Carrying it out in the real world

**First 100 days (presidential action, no Congress needed):**
- Treasury writes the Saver's Match rules (public comments [closed October 5, 2026](https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/insights/2026/09/guidance-issued-on-upcoming-savers-match-for-retirement-plan-contributions)) so there's a default destination for the money and claims are pre-filled.
- Treasury closes the Trump Account claim gap. Hospitals, Medicaid, WIC (the nutrition program for mothers and young children), schools and tax returns all become ways to claim, and the goal is starting deposits that need no app at all.
- The IRS stops losing enforcement staff, shifts auditors to partnerships and very wealthy filers, and publishes audit results every quarter.
- HUD and the Transportation Department use every bit of discretion in scoring grants, including the money ROAD lets them steer, to reward communities that permit housing.
- The scorecard launches: Treasury, the Fed and the Census Bureau publish a yearly Ownership Report, and an independent evaluator (GAO, Congress's auditing agency, or a National Academies panel) is named, with targets set in advance.

**Year one (the budget package):** Budget reconciliation can pass tax and spending changes with a simple majority, but its rules strip out anything that isn't about the budget. So the package is deliberately limited to taxes and deposits: step-up reform, the borrowing rule, guaranteed IRS funding, the permanent starting deposit and the yearly children's deposits, the Saver's Match fixes, and the trust fund. Here's the conflict you have to manage: reconciliation usually passes on party lines, and party-line laws get repealed. The best version is a negotiated deal with the other party's priorities written in: expanded Trump Accounts, the estate exemption kept, the Canada-style trade. Use party-line reconciliation only as the fallback.

**Years one to three (ordinary legislation, which needs 60 Senate votes):** the federal automatic IRA and public default account, housing conditions in the highway bill, and exempting these accounts from benefit-program asset limits. Each of these already has supporters in both parties, which is why they belong here rather than in the party-line package.

**Years three to eight and beyond:** evaluation, automatic adjustments and inflation indexing (see the next section).

**Who will fight it, and who will help.** Opponents: the estate-planning and wealth-management industry (over the step-up), local homeowner groups (over zoning), and parts of the financial industry that won't like a public default account competing with private IRAs. Allies: homebuilders; employers who can't hire because workers can't afford to live nearby; asset managers who gain money to manage from children's accounts; the "ownership society" right (Treasury Secretary Bessent's own phrase for these accounts); the wealth-gap left; faith groups; and governors who get more transportation money for building. The message has to be **"every American an owner,"** not "eat the rich." That isn't just spin. It's an accurate description of the plan, and it's the only message that holds a coalition together across a change in party.

## 6. How to make sure it actually works

Most big programs fail not because the idea is wrong but because nobody set up honest feedback, so they drift, get captured by interest groups, or quietly die. Seven safeguards guard against that.

1. **Set targets in advance, in law.** The targets and the evaluator are written into the statute before it passes, so nobody can move the goalposts later.
2. **Use the government's own account records, not just surveys.** Treasury holds every account balance and can report quarterly by income level and ZIP code. That's much faster than the Fed's wealth survey, which runs every three years.
3. **Test variations during the rollout.** Randomly vary the outreach channel (text messages, hospitals, tax returns) and the default settings across regions, and learn within months what gets more people enrolled.
4. **Build in automatic corrections.** If the projected median balance at 18 for kids from below-median-income families falls behind the target, the yearly deposit rises automatically. Metro areas that miss their permit targets lose a share of flexible federal money to areas that hit theirs.
5. **Protect the accounts.** Cap fees (around 0.1% a year), default to low-cost index funds, shield balances from creditors, exclude them from benefit-program asset limits, and keep predatory "advisers" away from the money.
6. **Make it hard to undo.** Universal, so everyone has a stake. Owned by individuals, so it's property rather than a program that can be cut. Funded by its own revenue stream, with credit to both parties. Social Security and the Affordable Care Act survived repeal attempts because of how many people relied on them, not because they were perfect.
7. **Kill what doesn't work.** Any part that misses its early indicators for three years in a row gets a required review and redesign. Programs that can't be shut down end up protecting their own budgets instead of producing results.

## 7. What most people get wrong

**Judging success by the top 1%'s share within one presidential term.** That number follows the stock market. Steer by ownership, bottom-half wealth after inflation, and balances at age 18.

**Believing that taxing the rich closes the gap by itself.** It lowers the top but builds nothing at the bottom unless the money is deliberately put into ownership. The connection between the two is the strategy.

**Designing programs people have to opt into.** The Trump Account claim gap (60 million-plus enrolled, about 8 million claimed) is a warning in real time. Make it automatic or accept that the people who most need it won't get it.

**Subsidizing home buyers without more homes.** The money flows straight into higher prices.

**Ignoring how much debt sits on the bottom half's wealth.** Pushing people into homeownership with thin equity is how the bottom half's share fell to 0.4% after 2008. Real equity has to come before ownership.

**Making it a partisan trophy.** Every part of this plan has to be something the next administration would rather take credit for than repeal.

**Expecting fast results.** The real payoff arrives in the 2040s. In the meantime, the political engine is the yearly statement every family receives showing their child's balance. That statement is what builds the base of supporters. It's how Social Security made itself untouchable.

---

## The bottom line

Make ownership automatic at the bottom, apply the same rules at the top, connect the two with a dedicated revenue stream, and build it so both parties can claim credit. Most of the pieces already exist. What's missing is the deposits that give more to poorer families, the automatic defaults, the trust fund, and the discipline to measure the right things.

The weakest link is housing. Zoning is local, and Washington can only pay for results, not order them. If I were stress-testing this plan further, that's where I'd spend the next hour: which metro areas, which formula, and how much federal money it takes to change a city council's vote. If useful, I can build a model of how children's account balances at 18 change under different deposit sizes and returns, or tighten this into a one-page memo.
